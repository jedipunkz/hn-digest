それを使うこととうまく使うことは別のスキルです
AI を実際に使用している人の中でも、スキルの差は非常に大きいです。自分が何をしているのか全く分かっていない AI のユーザーは、AI を導入した後は以前よりもはるかに悪い状況に陥っています。説明しましょう:
Claude をインストールし、それをリポジトリに指定し、4 単語のプロンプトを送信して、何かが起こるのを観察するのは簡単です (正しい場合もあれば、間違っている場合もあります)。
それをうまく使うことは別のスキルです。コンテキストをクリアするタイミングを知る。たった 2 回実行した内容は、プロンプトを再入力するのではなく、モデルが毎回読み取るスキル ファイルになるはずであることがわかっています。自動化プロジェクトのどの 15% が判断用のモデルを必要とするのか、どの 85% が決定論的なコードを必要とするのかを知ることができます。そして最も重要なことは、差分を受け入れる前に、差分を正しく読むことです。
同じチケットを 2 人のエンジニアに渡した場合、この相違は最初の 5 分で明らかになります。最初の方法では、Jira からのテキストを Claude に貼り付けて、送信をクリックするだけです。この修正は 6 つのファイルとこのエンジンに影響を与えます。

これをざっと読んで、テストがまだ合格していることを確認してから、PR をマージします。 3 週間後、本番環境が正常に動作し始めると、誰かがこの PR が理由もなく構成値を変更したことに気づくでしょう。
2 人目のエンジニアも同様に開始し、Jira からテキストを貼り付けますが、コードがリポジトリ内のどこに存在するか、どのフォルダーとファイルを触るか、放っておくかをフラグ付けします。また、クロードによって提出されたすべての PR が冗長ではなく最小限であることを確認するスキル ファイルがすでにあり、提出前に適切にテストされています。次に、差分を読み取り、迷走中の変更を見つけて、簡単なプロンプトで修正し、最初のエンジニアの半分のサイズの PR をマージします。
組織の少なくとも半分は、その 2 番目のバージョンに到達することは決してありません。 AI をうまく活用するのは技術です。 AI を本当に使いこなすには膨大な反復が必要であり、AI を使用していない人の惰性は多くの人にとって克服できません。スロップキャノンを洗練されたパワーユーザーに変えるのも同様に難しい。
完璧なロールアウトでもバーベルが生成されます
明らかな反対意見は、「まあ、これらの会社はひどいことをしている。私ならもっとうまくやるだろう。」というものです。いいえ、たとえ完璧に転がしたとしても、どうやってもバーベルを手に入れることになります。
私たちが話を聞いた別の幹部は、数千人規模の組織にエンタープライズ ライセンスを適用し終えたばかりでした。年間を通じて少なくとも 1,000 万ドルを費やすという約束。およそ 10% の人がトークンの 90% を消費します。考えてみると、これは基本的に図 2 の延長です。
これを大局的に考えると、組織全体が AI に関して優れていた場合、支出額は 1,000 万ドルではなく、1 億ドルになるでしょう。突然、最善のシナリオが最悪のシナリオになってしまいます。

AI Adoption is a Myth

vas on X: "https://t.co/haApURmzAN" / X Post
vas @vasuman AI Adoption is a Myth
You’re already in the top 1% of AI users. It might not feel like it because you see nerds on X spinning up agent fleets on a whim, but trust me, you’re in the top 1%. You’re likely using a model every day, you have opinions about how best to use it, and you maybe even tinker with agents or have set up Hermes on your own machine.
Yes, there’s a gap between you and the folks on the frontier at the hottest AI startups. The crazy kids running 20 terminals simultaneously, or a knowledge base that rewrites itself after every run with evals that self-optimize constantly.
The bad news is you’re never catching up to those people. The good news is that you don’t need to. That gap, the gap in front of you, is far smaller than the gap behind you.
Between you and the median employee at your company is a chasm. They have maybe opened ChatGPT 4 times in 2 years. They used 3.5-turbo and decided AI was stupid, not realizing that GPT 5.6 Sol is solving math problems that have been unsolved for decades. Every AI strategy article or post assumes that this person, this median employee, is going to eventually catch up.
For context, I’m the CEO of @varickagents. We work with the largest companies on the planet to implement AI agents and strategy across their organizations, so we know a thing or two about the reality of AI at enterprise.
The best AI tool in the world did not make them faster
I recently spoke to a leader of a non-technical enterprise’s operations org (thousands of people). Without AI, he said, his team was moving at a decent rate. No major complaints there.
Then they rolled out Claude Cowork, and what do you know? The team kept moving at the same rate. He asked me why.
I’ve seen the same split across every organization. Doesn’t even matter if it’s 50 people or 5000, it’s the same split every time: a barbell. 5-10% of the org were power users. They were using Cowork every day, leveraging skill files, connectors to Outlook, and more. These were the evangelists, the ones who pushed for Claude Cowork in the first place because they tinker with AI outside the job, and see first-hand how impactful it can be.
Of the other 90%, 20% would use it a couple of times a day, pretty poorly. They get some value out of it, but a fraction of what the frontier folk get. And the remaining 70%, they didn’t use it at all.
The dashboards in his org would indicate that the rollout counts as adoption. But to him, nothing got faster. Both of these were true simultaneously.
Using it and using it well are different skills
Even among those who do use AI, the skill gap is enormous. Users of AI that have no idea what they’re doing are actually far worse off after AI than they were before. Allow me to illustrate:
It is trivial to install Claude, point it at a repository, send a 4 word prompt, and watch something happen (could be right, could be wrong).
Using it well is a different skill. Knowing when to clear context. Knowing that the thing you just did twice should become a skill file the model reads every time, instead of a prompt you retype. Knowing which 15% of the automation project requires a model for judgement vs which 85% just requires deterministic code. And most importantly: reading a diff properly before you accept it.
If you give the same ticket to two engineers, this divergence becomes apparent in the first 5 minutes. The first one will simply paste the text from Jira into Claude and hit submit. The fix will touch six files, and this engineer will skim through this, see the tests still pass, and then merge the PR. Three weeks later, when production starts acting up, someone will realize this PR changed a config value for no reason.
The second engineer starts the same, they paste the text from Jira, but then they’ll flag where in the repository the code lives, what folders and files to touch vs to leave alone, and they already have skill files that ensure every PR submitted by Claude is minimalist instead of verbose, and is adequately tested before submission. They then read the diff, catch a stray change, fix it with a quick prompt, and then merge a PR half the size of the first engineer's.
At least half of any organization is never going to get to that second version. Using AI well is a craft. It takes enormous iteration to get really good at using AI, and the inertia of someone who isn't on AI is insurmountable for many. Turning a slop-cannon into a refined power-user is just as hard.
A perfect rollout still produces a barbell
The obvious objection is that “well these are companies doing it badly. I would do it much better.” No. Even if you roll it out perfectly, you'll get a barbell no matter what.
Another executive we spoke with had just finished putting enterprise licenses in front of their several-thousand-person organization. A commitment to spend at least $10M throughout the year. Roughly 10% of people burn 90% of the tokens. If you think about it, this is basically an extension of Figure 2.
To put that into perspective: if your entire org WAS great with AI, your spend would not be $10M, it would be $100M. All of a sudden your best-case scenario becomes your worst.
Adoption is binary, skill is a spectrum
McKinsey says 78% of organizations use AI in at least one business function, yet 80%+ of those same organizations report no significant EBIT impact. MIT NANDA's GenAI Divide report says 5% of integrated pilots produce over $1M in value, and the other 95% have nothing to show for it.
If you track adoption as a metric, you’re very likely asking (at most) an abstraction of a yes-or-no question. “Did this person log in this month?” “Did this person log in this year?” “Did this person send at least 5 prompts per day?” Etcetera. If you want to differentiate between "has never opened it" and "pastes emails in for reformatting" and "has three agents in production touching the general ledger," you need a better system of measurement. This is not the case for 99.9% of enterprise AI adoption trackers. This is stupid, for obvious reasons (see: barbell, above).
FIGURE 5 - What the adoption number hides
In all fairness, the number represents something real, and people are not lying about adoption. This is far easier to measure than the real thing you care about: how skillful is each person at using AI, and what is their ROI per token used?
Hence why AI adoption is a myth. If you actually tracked this metric, you would realize there is no hope for the vast majority of people to catch up to AI nativity in the enterprise. Does that mean they have no value? Of course not. It just means that your implementation and rollout plan needs to be adjusted accordingly.
Every incentive keeps the chasm open
Every AI vendor’s roadmap pitches increased capability for agents, but says nothing about the teams’ ability to use it effectively. So the only people who use it effectively are frontier users, who are the loudest, so they get their say. They’re also the most flattering to build for, so it’s a clear win-win. Every feature release raises the skill floor to use the product well, and this only widens the chasm between the frontier and the majority in an organization.
I keep seeing enterprises seduced by the idea of ‘train your employees how to prompt!’. This is 10% of the game. The other 90% is “train your employees to understand which workflows should never touch a model vs which workflows should be entirely automated”. The reason you can’t sell this as easily as the prompt-training is because the answer is different at every single company, and it takes a few weeks on-site to work out.
And the AI ninjas inside your company have no incentive to fix it either. Their leverage IS the chasm. They’re doing their work 70% faster, then get to coast. Or they do the work of 3 people. But the moment everyone’s on that wave, they lose their edge. Why would they fix it without additional incentive?
Well, you have to train anyway. You won’t even know who’s in the top slice vs who’s not without some training. There are definitely some people at your company who are interested in AI but lack the time and leverage to go deep. You should train to expose everyone’s ability and comfort level.
For your top slice, your power users, give them somewhere to publish, like a shared database, so that every skill they make gets posted, ranked, and installed by others. The gap still remains though: you can blast this to everyone and still 50% of people will never use a skill.
For everybody else, the work has to get done without them changing how they work, which means the AI goes into the background. Why do you need AI to be prompted by a human anyways? Just figure out what the most repetitive processes are, build agents in your existing systems of record that employees are used to (Salesforce, NetSuite, Dynamics, etc), and only pull in humans where you need a second glance.
For example: you have dozens of AP analysts who move invoices all day. 90% of this is automatable. You can’t expect them to spin up agents or prompt their way through it reliably without breaking in production. So build the agents that run every single day, and now these analysts are just approving, rejecting, or editing the work the agents do.
One thing you need to remember: people are not in the market for a tool that helps them get the work done. They just want the work done.

So stop reporting ‘adoption’ to your board. Report what share of the work today is manual, vs hybrid, vs fully automated. This is the only reporting and conversation that matters.
You’re likely in the top 1% and while there’s still a gap in front of you, there’s a chasm behind you. The median employee at your company is miles behind you, and the gap actually increases with every release.
The average Claude Cowork or similar rollout goes like this: 5-10% become power users, 20% use it poorly, 70% hardly use it, and nothing gets faster but you definitely burn millions of dollars.
Using a tool and using it well are separate skills, and at least half of any organization never uses it well.
A great rollout does not fix this. You can put AI in front of thousands, but 10% of your org will still burn 90% of your tokens.
Adoption metrics collapse entire spectrums of distribution into a binary yes and no, which is how you get figures like 78% adoption while 80%+ report no significant impact.
You need to train the top slice and publish what they build. For everyone else, put the AI in the background of the systems they already work in.
