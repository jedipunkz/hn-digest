Foxes, Lions, and LLMs: The Machiavellian Game of Tech Hiring

Beebs on X: "Every one sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are. https://t.co/9R8OqnwMj8" / X Post
Beebs @0xb33bs Every one sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are. Foxes, Lions, and LLMs: The Machiavellian Game of Tech Hiring
“Every one sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are.” — The Prince, Chapter XVIII
Imagine a developer in a Zoom interview smiling into the camera, effortlessly answering questions from a Principal Engineer. The interviewer smiles back. Every answer is technically sound and unusually well articulated.
What the Principal Engineer cannot see is the invisible overlay floating just outside the shared screen. It listens to each question and generates an optimal answer in seconds, just enough time for the developer to read and paraphrase before responding.
But the performance did not begin when the Zoom call started.
Let’s rewind a few weeks before the performance.
Perhaps the recruiter used an AI tool to find the applicant on LinkedIn and carefully crafted a personalized invitation to apply. If not, the applicant likely applied with their AI-curated resume, comparing keywords from the AI-generated job description with the AI prompts that the applicant used to carefully prepare their resume for the opportunity. “Think deeply and make sure my resume gets me to the next round.” The applicant carefully avoided the “write penguin in the text box below if you’re human” trap that was hidden in the applicant tracking system. Luckily, the candidate’s efforts paid off.
As a next step, thanks to ID verification services like Checkr, AI matches the candidate’s face with their ID to confirm that the applicant is a Midwestern dad rather than a deepfaked North Korean hacker. At this point, both parties in the interview have established their humanity, so the illusion of humanity can continue to the next stages.
The peculiar part of modern tech hiring is that both sides have fully adopted AI throughout the hiring process, while maintaining radically different rules about when to use it, when it must be disclosed, and what is considered cheating.
Machiavelli would have loved this game.
Interviews Were Already Theater
Tech interviews were already in a precarious state before AI. Developers know all too well the rituals before an interview. LeetCode grinding, system design flashcards, STAR stories, Glassdoor question banks, recruiter outreach, keyword optimization—all of these efforts extending on for months on end.
The interview, a culmination of these months of preparation, was an immense amount of power for an interviewer. The interviewer, the great decider, had the power to infer in just an hour, not only what a candidate is today, but what they can be. In less than a dozen questions, months of preparation could evaporate in front of a begrudging interviewer, who carefully crafted scripted descriptions of their team culture (“we move fast!”), the company (“world-class engineering”), the tech stack (you’ll code in English now anyway), and the coding question (a LeetCode medium, if you’re unlucky).
Thanks to LLMs, the emperor has no clothes. An interviewer’s power, just like a candidate’s coding ability, stands no chance against an LLM. The interviewer’s carefully copied-and-pasted, recycled LeetCode question can be solved in roughly three seconds in an optimal way by an LLM. The candidate’s performance has ironically become a literal acting performance.
The question is, how well can a candidate sell that they truly understand the code that the LLM writes?
“It is necessary to be a fox to discover the snares and a lion to terrify the wolves.”
Machiavelli uses a lion to represent strength and capability, while the fox recognizes traps and understands the game. Modern hiring now disproportionately favors the fox.
If you’ve ever entered LinkedIn, you’ve probably seen some variation of the following: a highly skilled developer, or product manager, or whatever it may be, has been looking for work for months or years. These kinds of posts perform incredibly well because one must think, when will the bell toll for me? However, from what I’ve observed in tech hiring, is that although these candidates may genuinely have excellent backgrounds, they are lacking some aspect of “The Game.”
The Game has changed faster than we could have imagined. It started as AI resume editing. Then, AI application answers. AI interview preparation. AI mock interviews. AI-assisted take homes. Visible copilots during coding exercises. Now, invisible overlays during live interviews.
How can a Principal Engineer at Renowned Tech Company, with thousands of hard-earned coding hours, and let’s say a generous typing speed of 85 words per minute, possibly compete with the new generation of players? Let’s put a Principal Engineer up against a Gen-Z computer science grad with a typing speed of 120 words per minute, an impressive reading ability (from thousands of hours of texting, of course), and a questionable moral compass (they are using a coding overlay during the interview). Who do you think will win at a 15-minute LeetCode game? What about a system design game? You might argue that the Principal Engineer could win. They very well might not.
During the interview, the interviewer thinks they are observing the candidate’s reasoning. During a PR review, the reviewer thinks they are observing the PR author’s reasoning. The LLM is now the man behind the curtain. There is no way to ascertain the LLM’s true depth of help that has been provided in either case. It is quite literally an acting performance. What makes a good actor?
Know your objective in every scene
Most importantly, you have to sell yourself as the character.
None of the above traits are what make a Principal Engineer great, but they sure make for a great interview. A charismatic actor who can do all of the above, with the intelligence of an LLM, is unstoppable. After all, an interview is just another sale, and the best salesman gets the job.
To put it bluntly, “Coffee is for closers.”
Companies increasingly claim the right to augment their own cognition with AI while expecting candidates to demonstrate their completely raw, human cognition during the interview process. Many job applications now explicitly include some kind of checkbox in which the candidate promises they definitely won’t use AI, or they’ll be forcibly taken out of the interview process, or even worse, blacklisted.
All of this effort is so the interviewer can ensure a “fair” interview process. The same interview process that includes AI candidate sourcing, AI job-description generation, AI applicant ranking and filtering, AI interview-question generation, AI interview transcription, AI note summarization, AI candidate evaluation and score, and the cherry on top, an automated rejection. It’s an incredible amount of AI outsourcing for a company whose Career page is plastered with smiling humans, and a list of the amazing benefits that their humans enjoy.
Why then, if employers are so set on selling the humanity of their company, are they outsourcing nearly their entire recruitment process to AI? It’s because a candidate’s use of AI is not seen as morally equivalent to a recruiter’s use of AI. After all, the interview is a test of the candidate’s ability, and the recruiter’s work is simply a mix of administrative work and some human taste.
But wait, developers don’t write code by hand anymore. In fact, it’s almost as if the developers are just applying their good judgement, their taste, to the code that the LLM generates. What else do developers do? Well, they know how to talk during meetings, organize resources, coordinate, and…other administrative work. Maybe a recruiter’s job and a developer’s job aren’t so different after all?
The Closed-Book Exam for an Open-Book Profession
Modern software development has always been an open-book exam. Documentation, code search, Stack Overflow, GitHub, coworkers, giant O’Reilly books, obscure last-page-of-Google PDFs in a different language, and anything else. Software developers are quite resourceful. Well, at least the best ones are. Yet, as developers, we have always faced the indignity of having these stripped away during interviews, and being forced to perform. Just our hippocampus in a war against our nerves for a six-figure tech job.
There is certainly value in foundational knowledge. If a developer doesn’t understand the concept of a database or an API, there’s no way they can prompt themselves out of that. That’s an awful low bar to set for ourselves though. What kinds of signals would be a good proxy for foundational knowledge these days?
Problem decomposition and articulation
Knowing when the model is wrong
Understanding the components of a codebase
Memorized syntax and live coding games are much less meaningful signals than the skills above. Now, gaming syntax and live coding has become effortlessly easy. It is much harder to game soft skills, because soft skills are a product of our personality, our values, and our humanity.
LLMs have given us the ultimate prisoner’s dilemma. If interviewing is a system, it is now terribly expensive to be honest. Both interviewers and hiring managers would prefer a world without deception. Nobody likes to lie (sociopaths may not mind it, though). However, now individual candidates are punished for their unilateral restraint in not cheating. The upside: a six-figure job, a happy and financially secure family. The downside: unemployment, an angry text from a hiring manager, and being on the blacklist of a random SaaS company.
“Whosoever desires constant success must change his conduct with the times.” — Machiavelli
I don’t want to live in a Machiavelli novel. Nor an Orwell novel. Nor any other novel that removes us from our humanity. The end should not justify a terrible means. We are at a crossroads in how we interview and how we hire. The Game, the current status quo, is based on deception—a game that rewards foxes and punishes lions. The future of interviewing shouldn’t be about deception or surveillance, but about genuine competence.
