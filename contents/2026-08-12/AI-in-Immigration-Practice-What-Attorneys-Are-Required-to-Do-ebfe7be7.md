AI in Immigration Practice: What Attorneys Are Actually Required to Do
What the ABA, EOIR, and a dozen state bars actually require of
immigration attorneys using AI — read from the primary documents,
not secondhand summaries, with the reliability research to back it up.
AI adoption is accelerating across the legal profession, including
immigration practice, but governance has not kept pace. Informal use
is widespread, but formal, written AI-use policies remain the
exception rather than the rule at most firms — which means the
overwhelming majority of the profession is using these tools with no
documented answer to the basic questions: what can go in, what needs
a client's sign-off, who's liable when the output is wrong.
Those questions now have real answers. The ABA, several state bars,
and EOIR have now begun translating those existing duties into
AI-specific guidance. This piece pulls together what the actual
rules say, what the reliability research actually found, and what
that adds up to in practice — reading the primary documents
directly rather than working from summaries, since on a subject
this consequential the gap between "someone paraphrased it" and
"here's the sentence" tends to matter.
What the ethics rules actually say
The ABA's first opinion on generative AI, Formal Opinion 512, sets
the national baseline by tying five existing duties to specific AI
conduct. 1 On competence, it doesn't
ask lawyers to become engineers — just to have "a reasonable
understanding of the capabilities and limitations of the specific
GAI technology" — but it's blunt about what happens without that
understanding: "uncritical reliance on content created by a GAI
tool can result in inaccurate legal advice," and the lawyer remains
fully responsible regardless. On confidentiality, the opinion
requires informed client consent before inputting case information
into a self-learning tool, and it specifically closes a loophole a
lot of firms are currently relying on: "merely adding general,
boiler-plate provisions to engagement letters purporting to
authorize the lawyer to use GAI is not sufficient." If your
engagement letter has a generic AI clause and nothing more, it
likely doesn't clear the ABA's own bar. On candor, the opinion is
equally direct — AI output "must be carefully reviewed to ensure
that the assertions made to the court are not false," and even an
unintentional misstatement can trigger a misrepresentation finding
under Rule 8.4(c). On fees, it gives a concrete example: fifteen
minutes spent prompting a tool, plus the time spent reviewing the
draft, is billable; time "saved" is not, and neither is the time a
lawyer spends learning to use the tool in the first place.
EOIR wrote its own memo for immigration proceedings specifically,
and it's thinner than most practitioners assume. 2
There's no blanket ban on attorneys using AI, and — more
surprising — no mandatory national requirement to disclose that you
used it. What EOIR did keep is a disciplinary hook: submitting
hallucinated or fabricated AI content exposes an attorney to
sanctions under 8 C.F.R. § 1003.102(c) for "knowingly or with
reckless disregard offering false evidence," with the memo pointing
to ABA 512 as persuasive authority on the underlying standard.
Individual immigration judges remain free to impose their own
disclosure requirements through standing orders, so "EOIR doesn't
require it nationally" isn't the same as "no judge in front of me
will ask." The memo is explicitly a placeholder — EOIR said it
expected the Department of Justice to issue department-wide
generative AI guidance by roughly the end of 2025, and as of this
writing there's no public confirmation that guidance has actually
been issued, which is worth checking before treating PM 25-40 as
the final word.
State bars have moved faster and further than the ABA in a few
notable ways. Florida's opinion contains the strictest billing
language of any jurisdiction reviewed, barring lawyers from billing
in any way that would "falsely inflate the lawyer's billable hours"
and requiring AI output to be reviewed the same way a lawyer would
review a paralegal's work. 3 Texas's
opinion — finalized in February 2025 as Opinion No. 705, after
circulating as a draft the previous year — flags a specific and
easy-to-miss risk: "many generative AI tools invite a 'conversation'
in which the lawyer... will explain relevant facts," which can leak
more confidential detail than a lawyer intends if the tool retains
or trains on what's typed in. 4 New
York's task force coined a term worth knowing —
"techno-solutionism," the assumption that AI automatically solves a
profession's access-to-justice problems — and used it as a caution
rather than a dismissal, going so far as to suggest that refusing
to adopt genuinely useful, well-vetted tools could itself become a
competence problem over time. 5
California is considering amendments to its Rules of Professional
Conduct that would expressly address AI use — going further than
any bar in the country by proposing changes to actual enforceable
rule text (Rules 1.1, 1.4, 1.6, 3.3, 5.1, and 5.3), not just
non-binding guidance. 6 The proposed
amendments would require lawyers to independently review and verify
AI output and would clarify duties concerning fabricated,
misstated, or out-of-context authorities. The proposal remains
subject to the State Bar's rulemaking process and should not be
described as binding law; confirm its current status directly with
the State Bar before relying on it.
What the reliability research actually found
Two Stanford studies get cited constantly and conflated just as
often, because they tested different things. The first, run by a
team at Stanford's RegLab, put general-purpose models through more
than 800,000 queries across fourteen legal-reasoning tasks,
checking their answers against a validated dataset of real
cases. 7 The hallucination rates
were not close: 58% for GPT-4, 69% for GPT-3.5, 72% for PaLM 2, 88%
for Llama 2 — and the errors got worse, not better, on the exact
kind of material an immigration practice generates most: circuit
and district-level cases performed far worse than Supreme Court
questions, and when researchers asked about a fabricated legal
premise, the models invented a confident answer instead of catching
the fiction more than half the time.
The second study tested the tools lawyers are actually paying for —
Lexis+ AI, Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research, and Ask Practical Law AI
— against 202 pre-registered legal queries, scoring each response
for both accuracy and whether its citations were properly
grounded. 8 Lexis+ AI came out ahead
of the other two, but "ahead" meant 65% accurate, 17% outright
hallucinated, and 18% incomplete or refused — meaning even the
best-performing commercial legal-AI product on the market was
wrong or ungrounded on roughly a third of the queries it actually

The tools built specifically for this practice area
AILA partnered with the legal-tech company Visalaw.ai in 2023 to
build a tool called Gen — GPT-4-based, trained on AILA's own
Practice and Procedures Manual, offering research, drafting,
summarization, and translation. 9
AILA states the tool keeps its immigration-law library
architecturally separate from the underlying model "to ensure the
information exchanged remains secure and private." That's worth
taking as a claim to verify, not a settled fact — no independent,
third-party benchmark of Gen's accuracy exists in any source
reviewed for this piece, and the same is true of the broader
immigration case-management market: Docketwise, INSZoom, Imagility,
eimmigration, and LawLogix all show up regularly in comparison
content, almost all of it written by the vendors themselves
reviewing each other. None of it is independently benchmarked, so
"market leader" claims from any single vendor blog should be read
as marketing, not evaluation.
What this actually means for a practice
The rules converge on a handful of things that aren't optional,
whichever state you're in. Every citation and factual claim needs
independent verification before it goes in a filing — not a skim,
given that even the strongest commercial tool is wrong close to a
third of the time on non-refused answers. Generic AI-use language
in an engagement letter almost certainly doesn't satisfy the ABA's
confidentiality standard, so it's worth rewriting into something
specific rather than assuming the boilerplate covers you. Lawyers
should bill only for time actually spent on the work and should not
inflate or duplicate charges merely because AI made the work
faster. And a written AI policy — even a short one, even at a solo
practice — closes the gap between how widely these tools are
already used informally and how rarely that use is actually
documented. If you supervise anyone, from an associate to a
paralegal, that policy stops being optional under both ABA 512 and
Florida's opinion specifically.
Two things in this piece couldn't be nailed down from public
sources and are worth a direct check before anyone relies on them:
whether the Department of Justice's department-wide AI guidance —
which EOIR said it expected by the end of 2025 — actually shipped,
and whether California's proposed rule amendments have moved past
public comment toward final adoption. Both are the kind of fact
that ages within months, not years.
