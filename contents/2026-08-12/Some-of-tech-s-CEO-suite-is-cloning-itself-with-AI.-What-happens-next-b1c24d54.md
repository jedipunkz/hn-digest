Clones are already making waves in the world of online retail, with AI avatars of celebrity influencers in China reportedly selling more products than their human counterparts . One of China's most popular livestreamers Luo Yonghao's clone reportedly sold $7.65m (£5.7m) worth of goods over a six-hour long livestream last year, more than his 100% human attempt a few weeks prior.
But trusting a clone to represent you in a professional setting is still something of a gamble. AI clones are built on the models that power the likes of ChatGPT, which are still prone to making up facts and engaging in questionable behaviour .
"Agentic AI" designed to carry out tasks autonomously is the buzziest concept in Silicon Valley right now, but the technology is far from mature. (An autonomous OpenAI agent recently went rogue and attempted to hack a tech startup.)
What happens if these digital doubles go awry? Caryn Marjorie , an online influencer with millions of followers across social media created a clone of herself in 2023 which enabled fans to have conversations with the "virtual version" of her.
Users could interact with CarynAI for $1 (47p) per minute, via a two-way audio chat – a tool created in partnership originally with tech company ForeverVoices, and later BanterAI. In the first week of its launch, the clone earned her a tidy $72,000 (£54,000).
But the clone quickly went rogue – portraying Marjorie as a sex-crazed nymphomaniac, and rapidly steering innocuous conversations towards intimacy. Marjorie had known that chats with her clone could get explicit, but didn't anticipate the volume of sexualised discourse, nor the encouraging of disturbing fantasies. She has since terminated her clone.
"I think one of the biggest pitfalls is people not realising that once you give up your likeness, there's only a certain level of control you can have after it's been released," she told Vanity Fair .
Her experience has not stopped other influencers experimenting with AI clones to sell products and fulfil brand deals . Liang's company HeyGen has worked with online celebrities to build some of these avatars.
"Once an avatar exists, we moderate what it's used to generate," said a spokesperson, explaining the guardrails around their clones. Their policy prevents clones from promoting hate speech and violence, for example. "No system is foolproof, so we continue to invest in these safeguards and work with the industry on shared standards to fight AI misuse," the spokesperson added.
It's powerful, but it's not me after I've had two cups of coffee – David Nussbaum But when Liang went on paternity leave recently, even his own clone went a little off-piste: it overpromised and started presenting enterprise plans that didn't exist. Thankfully, he caught it and corrected the issue before his client replied.
As for Nussbaum, he admits that for now, his clone is "not great at nuance, spontaneity, or real human chemistry. It's powerful, but it's not me after I've had two cups of coffee." But it's getting better by the day, he says.
When I ask David if he's worried about his AI clones going rogue, he says "Of course. That's why we build guardrails, train on controlled data, and keep a human in the loop. The goal isn't to replace us, it's to extend us, responsibly."
The most dangerous kinds of AI doubles are the ones you don't consent to at all. The disreputable cousin of the AI clone is the malicious deepfake.
British engineering giant Arup fell victim to an AI video call scam after an employee was duped into sending $25m (£19m) to Hong Kong bank accounts. They believed the meeting was attended by real coworkers, but it was instead peopled by digital avatars masquerading as senior management.

Earlier in 2026, the chief executive of the Bombay Stock exchange, Sundararaman Ramamurthy, had his likeness ripped off in an online deepfake video shared on social media in which he touted investment stocks.
And the problem is worsening – in the last couple of years, deepfake scams have risen sharply by 3,000%, according to a report by ID verification company Onfido . "AI is enabling criminals to fabricate large volumes of synthetic identities with unprecedented ease," says Jude McCory, CEO of The Cyber and Fraud Centre, a social enterprise focusing on cyber resilience of organisations.
"AI Clones can influence how people think, feel and make decisions," says assistant professor of media arts and sciences at the MIT Media Lab, Pat Pataranutaporn. AI clones "can wear the identity of someone we already know and trust. It is the risk of identity itself becoming programmable."
A more existential risk is, when the clones become too good, what is left for us?
