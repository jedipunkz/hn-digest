Does AI Improve Access to Justice?
What the controlled evidence shows about information,
representation, and outcomes — and why what people gained was
entry, not leverage.
There is a comfortable story about AI and access to justice.
Roughly 92% of the substantial civil legal problems of low-income
Americans get no or inadequate legal help. 1
Lawyers are scarce and expensive. AI is abundant and nearly free.
Therefore AI closes the gap.
Every step in that argument is defensible. The conclusion still
does not follow, and we now have enough evidence to say why.
The evidence assembled here points somewhere more uncomfortable
than either the optimistic or the pessimistic version of this
debate. It is not that AI produces bad legal information —
increasingly it does not. It is that legal information is
not the only, and may not be the binding, constraint once a
litigant enters the system. The controlled interventions
we found that supply information have increased procedural
participation without producing detectable improvements in case
outcomes. And representation — the intervention everyone assumes
works — turns out to work conditionally, in ways suggesting that
lawyers are not primarily delivering legal knowledge either.
Meanwhile, two studies of federal court records document the same
pattern since generative AI became widely available:
substantially more self-represented filing, without evidence of
improved litigation outcomes. 23 , 24
How these sources were selected. We ran a structured — not a
formal systematic-review-protocol — search across peer-reviewed
journals, working-paper repositories, research-institution
reports, court administrative data, and government statistics,
for evidence meeting three criteria: (1) it measures an outcome,
not a sentiment; (2) where it makes a causal claim, it uses a
design capable of supporting one, or the source itself disclaims
causal identification; (3) it is a primary source, or a faithful
report of one that we traced back to the original.
We separate findings into four evidence tiers, marked throughout:
primary source (government data, administrative
records, original survey instruments), peer-reviewed
research , working paper or preprint (not
yet peer-reviewed), and program or advocacy report
(typically no control group).
One further convention, which matters most in Parts 4 and 5:
claims that go beyond what a cited study states are marked
as interpretation. Everything else is a report of a source's own
finding. Where we draw an inference the underlying authors
did not draw, we say so and say why — and where an inference is
one of several readings the data supports, we give the
alternatives.
Two exclusions are worth stating. We excluded vendor benchmarks
and vendor-published performance claims entirely; they appear
nowhere in what follows. And we excluded program evaluations
reporting only volume served, satisfaction, or forms filed. Not
because those programs are unserious — because they do not answer
the question. That exclusion removed a substantial majority of the
published material we encountered.
Every figure in this article was verified against the primary
document, in its current version, in August 2026. Where a working
paper has been revised, we cite the current revision and say so.
Where a widely circulated figure could not be traced to a primary
source, we say that rather than repeating it — six such figures

than from body text, the source entry says so.
The funnel: what "improving access to justice" could mean
Almost every disagreement in this field is a disagreement about
which stage of a funnel is being measured. Setting the funnel out
first makes the rest of the evidence legible.
Problem → Recognition → Information → Action → Participation →
Legal position → Disposition → Real-world outcome → Welfare
Problem. A civil justice situation occurs.
Recognition. The person identifies it as something the legal system addresses.
Information. They learn what rules apply and what options exist.
Action. They file, answer, or otherwise engage.
Participation. They appear, respond to motions, meet deadlines.
Legal position. They have a defense, a claim, or a bargaining chip that carries weight.
Disposition. The case ends in a judgment, dismissal, or settlement.
Real-world outcome. They keep the housing, avoid the garnishment, obtain the status.
Welfare. They are better off, accounting for costs and any market response.
Representation, self-help materials, and AI are not stages — they
are interventions that enter the funnel at different points and
can only affect stages downstream of where they enter.
The funnel is an analytical decomposition, not a claim that every
case passes through these stages in sequence. Information can
produce recognition; a lawyer can produce both; leverage can
determine a disposition before any hearing occurs. The stages are
a vocabulary for asking what was measured , not a model of
how cases proceed.
What "outcome" means in this article
The word does most of the work in this literature and is used for
at least four different things. We distinguish them throughout:
Procedural outcome — an answer filed, an appearance made, a deadline met. Stages 4–5.
Legal outcome — a judgment, dismissal, settlement, possession order, or monetary recovery. Stage 7, also called a disposition .
Real-world outcome — housing retained, garnishment avoided, status obtained. Stage 8.
Welfare outcome — the real-world result net of costs, and of any market or institutional response. Stage 9.
Where a source says "outcome" without specifying, we say which of
these it measured.
The pattern this article documents is that most claims
about AI and access to justice are supported at stages 2 through
5, while the evidence that matters sits at stages 7 through
9. Movement at an early stage does not propagate
automatically to a later one.
Part 1: The gap is real, and it is not shaped the way most people think
The headline number is sound. The Legal Services Corporation's
2022 Justice Gap study — a NORC AmeriSpeak probability-panel
survey fielded 18 October to 4 December 2022, covering 2,003
adults at or below 125% of the federal poverty line plus 3,305
above it, alongside a four-week census of requests for help across
LSC-funded organizations — found that low-income Americans get no
or inadequate legal help for 92% of their
substantial civil legal problems. LSC-funded organizations turn
away 49% of eligible requests due to limited
resources. 1
(Primary source. There is no 2025 or 2026 edition.)
Two more recent findings complicate the standard framing.
The gap is not confined to poverty. The State Bar
of California's 2024 Justice Gap Study — over 6,000 respondents,
fielded 8 August to 7 October 2024, published 2025 — found that
between 72% and 75% of households in every income
group experienced at least one civil legal problem, and
that between 84% and 86% of civil legal problems in every
income group received no or inadequate
help. 2
(Primary source.) The income bands run from at-or-below
200% of the federal poverty line to above 120% of area median
income. On the narrower substantial-impact measure the
spread is wider and non-monotonic — 79%, 76%, 73%, 78% — which
still supports the point.
One comparison to avoid, and we flag it because it is tempting:
California's 85% is not comparable to LSC's 92%. LSC's figure
covers substantial-impact problems; California's comparable
substantial-impact figure is 77% . The 85% covers
all civil legal problems. These are California figures, not
national ones.
A legal right to counsel does not produce counsel.
New York City has a statutory right to counsel in eviction
proceedings. The NYC Comptroller's May 2025 report found the share
of eligible tenants receiving full representation fell from
71% in the fourth quarter of FY2021 to 42% in the fourth
quarter of FY2024 , with a monthly low of 30% in
March 2025 ; in the Bronx, from 81% to 31% over the same
quarters. 3
(Primary source — government oversight report.)
The methodology deserves a note, because it cuts both ways. The
Comptroller counts only cases with two or more appearances, which
excludes the fastest-disposed cases where representation is
lowest. But it also marks an entire case as represented if counsel
appeared at any single appearance. Both choices inflate the
figure. True representation across all filed cases is plausibly
lower than 42%.
Capacity, not entitlement, is the operative constraint.
Six numbers you will see cited that do not survive checking
This field has an unusually severe zombie-statistic problem.
Anyone writing about it should know which numbers are contaminated
— and in four of these six cases, the problem is worse than the
standard correction suggests.
1. "86% of civil legal problems get inadequate or no
help." This is LSC's 2017
figure. 4 The 2022 figure is
92%. 1 The persistence of this
number is not a media failure: LSC's own website currently
presents the 2017 figures in the present tense with no
date qualifier — "In the past year, 86% of the civil legal
problems reported by low-income Americans received inadequate or
no legal help." 5 The institution
that produced the replacement is still serving the original,
undated.
2. "80% of the civil legal needs of the poor go
unmet." LSC never published this figure. The 2009 report
says "less than one in five low-income persons get the legal
assistance they need." 6 The 80% is
a rounded restatement — and it rounds the wrong way, since "less
than one in five" implies more than 80% unmet.
3. "One legal aid lawyer for every 10,000 low-income
Americans." We could not trace this to any primary
source, and it appears to be a denominator-swap artifact. The
ABA's 2023 Profile of the Legal Profession reports roughly
three civil legal aid lawyers per 10,000 people in
poverty — about one per 3,333 — with a state range from
about 1.1 per 10,000 in Mississippi and South Carolina to 7.2 in
New York. 7 The circulating figure
appears to be the ABA's denominator with the numerator collapsed.
4. "Only 37% of immigrants have a lawyer; 14% of detained
immigrants." These are 2007–2012 figures from Eagly and
Shafer's 2015 study. 9 The 2025
update by Eagly, Shafer and Moulton — analyzing nearly 8 million
cases — puts representation in merits completions at
52% for 2013–2024 . 8
(Peer-reviewed research on government administrative data. The
authors state: "while we continue to show robust and statistically
significant correlations between representation and case outcomes,
we do not argue that representation caused these results.")
The genuinely alarming current figure is a different one:
representation among pending cases fell from
47% in 2012 to 31% in 2024 . 8
5. "Self-representation exploded between 1992 and
2013." Engstrom and Engstrom show the endpoints are not
comparable: the 1992 study excluded limited-jurisdiction and
small-claims courts, which account for 45.1% of
the 2013 sample (417,063 of 925,344 cases), and the two studies
used opposite coding presumptions for whether a party counted as
represented. 10 The apparent
explosion is at least partly a measurement artifact.
6. "90% of landlords have lawyers; 10% of tenants
do." No national source exists. The most widely
circulated version — "only 4% of tenants are represented
nationwide, compared to 83% of landlords" — appears in the
National Low Income Housing Coalition's Advocates' Guide
with no citation or footnote . 11
Every variant we traced (90/10, 83/4, 81/3) is an uncited
secondary assertion. There is no national eviction-representation
census. Jurisdiction-specific data exists and should be cited in
its place.
