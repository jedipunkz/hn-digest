メタのプロジェクトは、なぜこの問題が緊急を要するのかを示しています。同社の計画では、エドモントンの北約 35 キロメートルに 1 ギガワットのキャンパスを建設する予定で、これがカナダ初のメタ データセンターとなります。メタは、建設のピーク時に約 3,000 人の労働者、施設が稼働したら 300 人以上の常用雇用を計画しています。また、地元の道路や水道のインフラ整備に約6,000万ドルを投入した。
アルバータ州は、このプロジェクトに関連する使用料、税金、課徴金、送電料金が年間およそ 2 億 5,000 万ドルになると予測しています。これらの数字は経済に関する強力な宣伝になりますが、依然として予測にとどまります。建設業の仕事は一時的なもので、恒久的な雇用ははるかに少なく、公的給付はキャンパスが計画された規模に達するかどうかによって決まります。

That public case arrives at a sensitive moment. Meta Platforms Inc. ’s $13 billion AI data centre in Sturgeon County is already under construction, and a recent protest in Morinville brought concerns about water, electricity costs, environmental effects and consultation into sharper view. Alberta has now launched a new information website and a series of public town halls.

Those meetings will explain Alberta’s data centre strategy and gather public input. They are not approval hearings for Meta’s campus or any other individual project. Each development can face a different mix of municipal, utility, water and environmental decisions.
Meta’s project shows why the subject has become urgent. Company plans call for a one gigawatt campus about 35 kilometres north of Edmonton, making it the first Meta data centre in Canada. Meta projects around 3,000 workers at the peak of construction and more than 300 permanent jobs once the facility is operating. It has also committed about $60 million for local road and water infrastructure.
Alberta forecasts roughly $250 million a year in royalties, taxes, levies and transmission fees connected with the project. Those figures make a powerful economic pitch, but they remain projections. Construction jobs are temporary, permanent employment is much smaller, and public benefits will depend on whether the campus reaches its planned scale.
Power may be the most difficult part of the wider strategy.

Interest from developers has moved far beyond what Alberta’s grid can absorb at once. Provincial figures based on the July 30 connection list put requested data centre load at about 19,565 megawatts, more than the highest demand the province has recorded in a single hour. Many requests are preliminary and may never become operating projects, but the total shows why every proposal cannot connect.
Alberta Electric System Operator set an interim limit of 1,200 megawatts for the first round. A 970 megawatt Meta related load and a 230 megawatt Keephills project received that allocation and signed load contracts. Both still have to proceed through the connection process, while other requests move into the next phase.
Government policy favours projects that bring new power generation or storage. Meta’s campus is paired with a privately financed natural gas power plant planned next door and a grid connection. Premier Danielle Smith says data centres will pay for their own electricity, connections and infrastructure.

That is the province’s policy promise. Whether it works as intended will depend on contracts, regulatory decisions and each project’s final design. Alberta expects Meta’s transmission payments to reduce the transmission portion of household bills, but that is a government forecast, not a guaranteed reduction in every family’s total bill.
Water raises a similar need for project specific answers.
Meta says sealed liquid loops and dry cooling will avoid water use for routine cooling at its Sturgeon County campus. Water will still be needed for workers, fire protection and equipment maintenance. Meta compares its projected annual operational use with less than one typical golf course.
Future proposals may use different technology or seek water from different sources. Alberta’s Water Act requires authorization for surface or groundwater use, while a developer could also pursue a transferred licence or municipal supply where available. Residents directly affected by an application can file a statement of concern during the public notice period.
Local unease is not limited to resource use. Some Sturgeon County residents have said they learned too little before construction began. County officials have pointed to earlier public opportunities when data centres were added to local land use rules. Following a legal process does not always leave residents feeling meaningfully consulted.
Alberta’s town halls may answer broad questions, but formal influence usually comes through other channels. Municipal councils handle zoning and development decisions. Alberta Utilities Commission reviews power plants. Environmental and water applications have separate notice periods and appeal rules.
A separate Crossfield area proposal showed that local reaction can still alter plans. Its developer withdrew the site this spring after opposition, without either Crossfield or Rocky View County formally rejecting it.
Alberta has moved quickly from attracting investment to managing public expectations. Economic opportunity is easy to describe: private capital, construction work, taxes and a new market for provincial energy. Harder questions are local and specific. Residents want to know what will be built, what resources it will use, who pays if plans change and when their input can affect the result.
Town halls can begin that conversation. Confidence will depend on what happens afterward, when broad promises meet individual project filings.
