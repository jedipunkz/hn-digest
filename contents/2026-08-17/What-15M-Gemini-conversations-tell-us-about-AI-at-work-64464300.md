生産性統計を除いて、コンピューター時代はあらゆるところで目に見えました。研究者らは、「AIはどこにでも存在しているように見えるが、雇用、生産性、成長に関する従来の多くの尺度では、その影響を認識するのが依然として難しい」と述べている。
このギャップに対処する Google の試みは、Gemini アプリ、Google の AI モード、Gemini API にわたる 1,500 万件の匿名化された AI インタラクションのデータに基づいており、これらを合わせて毎月 10 億人以上が使用しています。研究者らの最初のレビューでは、AIはさまざまな職業にわたって重要な利用が見られるものの、その利用は「本質的に浅く、圧倒的に協調的なものにとどまっており、エンドツーエンドのタスク自動化の範囲は限られている」ことが判明した。
研究者らは、労働統計局の標準職業分類と、O*NET の特定の仕事のやり取りに関するより詳細なデータベースを使用して、自動分類器を使用して「仕事」タスクと「非仕事」タスクを分割しました。人間のレビュー担当者による検証により、Gemini プロンプトが仕事にどのように使用されているかを示す信頼できる尺度であることがわかりました。
米国の労働市場を説明する約 19,000 の個々の O*NET タスクのうち、意味のあるものとして登録されるのに十分な量の Gemini アクティビティ (タスクごとに世界中で少なくとも 25 回のユーザー インタラクションが試みられたもの) が見られるのは、わずか約 20% だけです。
米国の人口雇用と比較したジェミニの使用状況を調べたところ、AI の使用が最も多い職業はコンピューターと数学、法務、管理であることが明らかになりました。
「AIの高度な使用が観察されるこれらの職業の一般的なタスクには、データの収集と分析、情報の監視、コンピューティング環境の維持、科学的分析に関連するソフトウェアの開発、ソフトウェアのテストまたは検証が含まれます」と研究者らは述べています。
ソフトウェア開発者やシステム管理者もその中に含まれます。

雇用に占める割合と比較して、最も多く存在する職業。研究者らは、特に「コーディングなどの重要な作業ユースケースを適切に把握するために」、API トラフィックを含めるように注意しました。
どの職業が最も浸透しているかを見ると、ソフトウェア品質保証のアナリストとテスター、ネットワークおよびコンピュータ システムの管理者が、人事担当者や文書管理の専門家と並んでリストの上位近くに位置しています。
AI が広く存在しているからといって、必ずしも現在の使用法が普及していることを意味するわけではありません。詳細な職業の実質 68% で何らかの使用が見られ、米国の雇用の 88% をカバーしています。
しかし、使用量が示されている職業の中でも、中央値はその職業のタスクの 21% に過ぎません。非定型のコグニティブ AI 会話のうち自動化の意図を示すのは 10% 未満で、残りは草案、レビュー、アイデア作成、検索に分かれています。日常的な認知作業は例外です。そこでは、会話の 4 分の 1 以上が完全な自動化を目的としています。
全体として見ると、データは漸進的な変化を描いています。AI は依然として、日々のワークロードのごく一部を処理する部分的な協力者にすぎません。それは部門間で不均等に展開され、特定のタスクに集中しており、人間の生産力の代替としてではなく、人間の生産量を増幅するものとして圧倒的に機能しています。
タスクの支援からジョブの自律実行への飛躍には、現在のシステムが単独で実行することができない深いドメイン コンテキスト、判断、検証が必要です。大多数の知識労働者にとって、AI はハンドルを握っていません。それは、いくぶん気まぐれなパワーステアリング システムを提供しているだけです。

Google’s study of 15 million Gemini interactions finds AI usage stays shallow : even in adopting occupations, the median is just 21% of tasks touched.
AI amplifies human work rather than replacing it .
The data points toward a “runaway” scenario where experienced workers pull further ahead rather than juniors catching up.
It’s a stressful time to be a knowledge worker – anyone whose job relies on thinking and communicating rather than manual labor. The claims are widespread: AI could surpass most humans at most things by 2028, and has the theoretical capability to displace large swaths of the workforce . However, a new Google research study , the ‘AI & Economy ATLAS’, tells a different story.
We should note that this is Google’s own data about its own product. It has not been independently audited, and it explicitly excludes paid/enterprise Gemini API and Google Cloud/Workspace usage, where a lot of professional engineering work happens. In addition, the report itself is based on interaction volume, and does not show productivity outcomes. Despite these shortcomings, however, Google’s research offers us a useful snapshot.
How to avoid knowledge decay in software engineering
Observability tools weren’t built for AI debugging
It opens with a new variant on the economist Robert Solow’s quip, sometimes referred to as the Solow Paradox , that the computer age was visible everywhere except the productivity statistics. “AI appears to be everywhere,” the researchers state, “yet its impact remains hard to discern in many traditional measures of employment, productivity, and growth.”
Google’s attempt to address the gap is based on data from 15 million anonymized AI interactions across the Gemini App, Google’s AI Mode, and the Gemini API, which together are used by more than 1 billion people monthly. The researchers’ initial review finds that, while AI sees some significant use across a wide variety of occupations, usage “remains shallow and overwhelmingly collaborative in nature, with end-to-end task automation limited in scope.”
The researchers used an automated classifier to split ‘work’ and ‘non-work’ tasks using the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Standard Occupational Classification and O*NET’s more detailed database of specific work interactions. Verification by human reviewers found it to be a reliable gauge of how Gemini prompts were being used for work.
Of the roughly 19,000 individual O*NET tasks that together describe the US labor market, only around 20% see enough Gemini activity, with a minimum of 25 attempted user interactions globally per task, to register as meaningful.
Looking at Gemini usage relative to population employment in the US revealed that the occupations with most AI use are Computer and Mathematical, Legal and Management.
“Common tasks in these occupations where we observe high AI usage involve data collection and analysis, information monitoring, maintaining computing environments, developing software related to scientific analysis, and testing or validating software,” the researchers state.
Software developers and systems administrators are among the most over-represented occupations relative to their share of employment. The researchers were careful to include API traffic, specifically “in order to adequately capture key work use cases like coding.”
When you look at which occupations have the deepest penetration, software quality assurance analysts and testers, and network and computer systems administrators sit near the top of that list, alongside human resources and document management specialists.
The widespread presence of AI does not necessarily mean that its current usage is pervasive. A substantial 68% of detailed occupations show some usage, covering 88% of US employment.
Yet, even within occupations that show usage, the median is just 21% of that occupation’s tasks. Under 10% of non-routine cognitive AI conversations show automation intent, with the rest split across drafting, review, ideation, and retrieval. Routine cognitive work is the exception: there, more than a quarter of conversations are aimed at outright automation.
When viewed as a whole, the data paints a picture of an incremental shift: AI remains a partial collaborator handling a narrow sliver of the daily workload. It is deployed unevenly across sectors, concentrated in specific tasks, and functioning overwhelmingly as an amplifier of human output rather than a substitute for it.
The leap from assisting with a task to autonomously executing a job requires deep domain context, judgment, and verification that current systems simply cannot perform on their own. For the vast majority of knowledge workers, AI hasn’t taken the steering wheel; it has merely provided a somewhat capricious power-steering system.
The disconnect between expectations and engineering reality
This shallow, collaborative reality may help to explain why developers and their bosses disagree over generative AI . As other surveys highlight, top-down mandates often focus on writing code rather than solving the broader friction across the software delivery lifecycle. In fact, when tools are applied recklessly, developers report spending more time debugging generated code and fixing security vulnerabilities than shipping value.
Furthermore, as AI makes generating code virtually free, engineering teams are running straight into what I call the AI code verification trap . AI made code cheap; verification is now expensive. When PR volumes surge, traditional human code review becomes a bottleneck. Staring at machine output all day to catch subtle, plausible-sounding errors can cause engineers to burn out and convert creative problem-solvers into assembly-line QA checkers. Development team leads need to be cognizant of these risks.

There is also an inequality question. Google’s research lays out two scenarios. In the ‘catch-up’ scenario, broad, well-supported adoption lets less experienced workers close the gap with experts. In the ‘runaway’ scenario, adoption stays concentrated among people who are already advantaged, and the gap widens, which is what the report’s own real-world usage data, and other observational studies it cites, suggest may be happening today.
Of course this idea that if you are not already adopting AI you are being left behind is an old marketing trick to try and push adoption. FOMO is real for execs, as it is for everyone else, but there is a risk that junior engineers may apply AI to the wrong tasks or in the wrong way, while senior engineers already know which problems are worth handing off. Getting value out of AI-generated code still largely depends on human code-reading ability and architectural judgment to catch errors.
If senior developers are more effective at coding with AI tools , organizations may lean on them more instead of hiring and training juniors. However, that would be shortsighted, since AI significantly reduces the time needed for junior developers to become effective.
I noted a couple of important caveats with this research earlier on, specifically that this is Google’s own data about its own product. It has not been independently audited, and it explicitly excludes paid/enterprise Gemini API and Google Cloud/ Workspace usage.
I should note another, which is that the technology is young, and we are all trying to get to grips with it. Between 2014 and 2020, when I was lucky enough to be InfoQ’s chief editor, I had the privilege of talking to thousands of developers from the keynote stage at QCon multiple times a year.
My pitch, which was mainly about encouraging developers to write , was that developers and IT industry leaders should “share what they’ve learnt, as this is how our industry moves forward.” Now back in the trenches in the age of AI exploration, that message feels more important than ever.
