アオサギ ( Butorides virescens ) には 3 本の足がありますか?いいえ！しかし、Google の Gemini AI はそう考えているようです。 eBird チェックリストに提出されたこの実際の写真が改ざんされました。
eBird、Macaulay Library、iNaturalist などのコミュニティ主導の科学プロジェクトでは、AI によって操作された写真や AI によって生成された写真が急速に増加しています。 Nature の新しい出版物は最近、これらのプロジェクトに対する AI 写真の脅威を強調し、いくつかの人気ニュース記事につながりました。私たちは、AI が写真の科学的価値をどのように低下​​させるかについての意識を高め、コミュニティに知らせたいと考えています。
AI は私たちの日常生活にますます浸透しており、ほぼすべての最新テクノロジーやお気に入りのアプリケーションに、オプションのものもあれば、避けられないものもあるさまざまな形式の AI が追加されています。 AI を使用すると、画像に詳細を追加したり削除したりして、野鳥観察中の鳥の実際の姿を表さない画像に変更してしまう可能性があります。これは、3 本目の脚が追加されたアオサギの場合のように極端な場合もあれば、画像をより鮮明に見せるために偽の羽のディテールを作成するなど、より微妙な場合もあります。さらに厄介なことに、多くの人は、AI が自分の写真に加えている変化の程度にさえ気づいていない可能性があります。なぜなら、一見すると写真が「より良く」、より鮮明で、鮮明に見えるだけかもしれないからです。さらに、多くの携帯電話や編集アプリケーションは、写真処理に AI を使用するように変更されていますが、多くの場合、ユーザーの知らないうちに、AI を無効にするオプションがありません。
次の例は、この問題について論じた最近の Nature 記事からのものです。エポーレットオリオールの元の画像 (画像 B) をジェミニ 3 号が品質を「改善」するために編集したところ、C) 画像が得られました。

これは、同じ属ではない別の限界外種であるアカハネクロウタドリにほぼ完全に似ており、専門の鳥類学者とコンピューター ビジョン画像分類者の両方をだますことが予想されます。
最近の Nature 記事からの大規模言語モデルの画像生成と操作の例: 「市民科学プラットフォームは生成 AI の脅威を軽減する必要がある」 ( Lees et al. 2026 )。 A) Gemini 3 (Google) で「100 m の距離から 500 mm の望遠レンズで撮影したかのように、地衣類で覆われた細い枝に左を向いて座っているオスのアカハネクロウタドリのフォトリアリスティックな画像を作成してください。鳥は尻尾を押さえています。後ろの空は明るい青色です。」というプロンプトを使用して作成された画像。この画像は、アカハネクロウタドリ Agelaius phoeniceus (北米に一般的な種) を説得力をもって表現したもので、「ディープフェイク」画像であるという明らかな兆候はありませんが、尾羽の数が予想される 12 枚よりも少ないように見えます。 B) マコーレー図書館所蔵の画像 (ML597448011) の、南アメリカの熱帯雨林種であるエポーレットオリオール黄疸のカヤネンシス。ブラジルのアマゾンで撮影されたもの（写真家デイビッド・テオバルド）。 C) 「これをより良く見せてください」というプロンプトを使用して Gemini 3 (Google) で生成された ML597448011 の操作画像。
マコーレー図書館は、科学的研究や将来の研究と応用のために、デジタル メディアを通じて自然界を文書化することを目的としています。そのため、eBird を通じて提出された写真は、種が発生する場所と時期、その行動、見た目、生息環境の記録とみなされます。AI によって導入された変更はその自然の真実を改変し、他の鳥観察者や科学コミュニティの間での ML アーカイブの信頼性を損ないます。

From Truth to Fake: The Threat of AI to Media Archives
Do Green Herons ( Butorides virescens ) have three legs? No! But Google’s Gemini AI seems to think so. It altered this actual photo submitted to an eBird checklist.
Community-driven science projects such as eBird, the Macaulay Library, and iNaturalist are seeing a rapid uptick in AI-manipulated and AI-generated photos. A new publication in Nature has recently highlighted the threat of AI photos to these projects, leading to some popular news articles . We want to raise awareness and inform the community of how AI reduces the scientific value of photos.
AI is increasingly being pushed into our daily lives—nearly every updated piece of technology and your favorite applications have added various forms of AI, some optional and some unavoidable. Use of AI can add or remove details from images, changing your image into something that does not represent what the bird actually looked like while birding. This can be extreme, as in the case of the Green Heron where a third leg was added, or be more subtle, such as creating false feather detail to make a picture appear sharper. Even more troubling, many people may not even realize the extent of changes that AI is making to their photos, since they may just appear “better”, clearer, and sharper at first glance. Furthermore, many phones and editing applications have changed to using AI for photo processing, often without your knowledge and with no option to disable it.
The following example is from a recent Nature article discussing this problem. When the original image of the Epaulet Oriole (image B) was edited by Gemini 3 to ‘improve’ its quality, it resulted in C) an image that almost perfectly resembles a different extralimital species which is not in the same genus—the Red-winged Blackbird—and would be expected to fool both expert ornithologists and computer vision image classifiers.
An example of large language model image generation and manipulation from a recent Nature article: “Citizen science platforms must mitigate against the threat of generative AI” ( Lees et al. 2026 ). A) Image produced with Gemini 3 (Google) using the prompt “Produce a photo-realistic image of a male Red-winged Blackbird, facing left sat on a thin lichen-covered branch as viewed from below as if taken with a 500 mm telephoto lens from a distance of 100 m. The bird is holding its tail down. The sky behind is bright blue.” This image is a convincing representation of a Red-winged Blackbird Agelaius phoeniceus (a common North American species) which has no obvious signs of being a ‘deepfake’ image – although it looks like it may have fewer than the expected 12 tail feathers. B) Image from the Macaulay Library (ML597448011) of an Epaulet Oriole Icterus cayanensis , a South American rainforest species, taken in Amazonian Brazil (photographer David Theobald). C) Manipulated image of ML597448011 produced with Gemini 3 (Google) using the prompt ‘make this look better’.
The Macaulay Library aims to document the natural world through digital media for scientific studies and future research and applications, and as such photos submitted to it through eBird are considered a record of where and when species occur, their behavior, what they look like, and the habitat they occur in. AI-introduced changes alter that natural truth, eroding the credibility of the ML archive among other birders and the scientific community.
Use of AI to generate or alter photos submitted to eBird checklists and Macaulay Library is strictly prohibited. For photos you are submitting to eBird checklists, don’t use AI to process them and avoid using AI-powered tools within your preferred photo editing software. This might include buttons/tools like “Improve”, “Upgrade”, “Enhance”, or “Generate” in common photo editors. Also, be aware that some newer phones will automatically use AI to sharpen very zoomed-in photos.
Don’t put AI-manipulated photos into eBird checklists.
Don’t use AI chatbots or generative AI tools to “enhance” or “improve” photos for your eBird checklists.
Don’t overuse AI-powered editing tools on photos you upload to eBird; avoid using them at all when possible.
Don’t use AI to generate images of what you thought you saw as documentation in an eBird checklist.
Do keep your photos as natural as possible.
Do retain the original metadata for your photos when editing and exporting.
Do describe what you saw with words, especially if you didn’t get a photo.
Read our full AI policy . Individuals repeatedly found to be uploading AI-generated or significantly altered images may have their accounts set to Not Public.
What is eBird and the Macaulay Library doing to help?
In the coming weeks, tags will be added to photos, video, and audio submitted to the Macaulay Library through eBird that are found to be AI-manipulated or over-edited. These tags will help eBird and Macaulay maintain the integrity of the archive and clarify our media-editing policy.
Artificial tags will be added to media determined to be fully or substantially AI-generated, or edited so extensively that it no longer reliably depicts the organism, sound, or environment. Artificial media will be fully hidden from all public output.
Over-edited tags will be added to media determined to be edited beyond the limits described in our guidelines but will remain publicly accessible, although they might be excluded from some research projects and might not be used to improve Merlin or other Lab tools.
New tags in the Macaulay Library will indicate when a media item has been over-edited or artificially generated.
These tags will be applied as new media are found or brought to our attention. If you see these tags on your own media, you can 1) replace it with a less edited or non-AI version and/or 2) leave it be but change your editing workflow to avoid the same issue for your future uploads.
Please follow our best practices outlined above and be sure your photos accurately reflect what the bird looked like in the field. AI tools that generate content or change existing details are never acceptable. Even AI-powered editing tools like noise reduction and sharpening should be used only lightly, if at all. An image with artificial backgrounds or features might be nice to look at, but it is not scientific data and should not be uploaded to eBird and the Macaulay Library.
In the future, the eBird community may also be able to flag this type of content directly. In the meantime, we encourage you to help identify media that is potentially artificial or over-edited by notifying us using this Google Form .
Examples of AI photos in Macaulay
Below we outline the main ways that AI usage can lead to wholly or partially falsified data using actual examples of photos submitted to Macaulay Library:
Even without AI, photographers have long been able to manipulate their photos using various editing software. When editing, the goal should always be to have a natural reproduction of how the bird looked in life. Overuse of any editing tool, regardless of AI, can also result in very strange-looking birds. We ask eBirders to avoid over-saturation, over-sharpening, or augmenting the photo beyond what you observed.
Recent advancements in AI tools in commonly used photo editing software take this to the extreme, such as AI sharpening and denoise tools adding fake “clarity” to bird feathers.
Example 1. Over-editing with AI tools. AI sharpening and denoise tools are being overused on ML media. A natural, scientifically valuable photograph of a Black-chested Mountain Tanager ( Cnemathraupis eximia; top) was over-edited using AI tools resulting in false detail to the bird’s feathers and beak as well as the branch it is perched on (bottom).
Photographers should never turn any editing settings to the max, like shadows, sharpness, and brightness, as they are guaranteed to create an unnatural looking image. Even default levels of AI denoise are likely to introduce fake details.
People seeking to “enhance” or “improve” their photographs using AI chatbots like Google Gemini are almost guaranteed to inadvertently remove natural details like insects and plants while adding fake details like new color patches or changes to beak morphology. For example, we’ve seen an uptick in people asking AI chatbots to “make this photo better” or similar prompts. Remember that these AI tools are a “black box”—you have no idea what they are doing to your photo and you have little to no control over it, so don’t use them for eBird photos! Removing branches and using any type of AI generative fill to replace the branch or completely replacing the background of a photo also fabricates information and should never be done. It’s okay to have a branch in front of the bird!
Example 2 . AI enhanced . Top: Original photo of a Grasshopper Sparrow ( Ammodramus savannarum ). Bottom: “Enhanced by Gemini AI” from the top photo.
At first glance the bottom picture above may appear identical and cleaner. But if you look closely, you’ll notice that Gemini changed critical biological information in the picture. Most importantly, the enhanced image removes the food the sparrow had in its bill (which removes evidence of the type of food the sparrow eats and potential evidence of breeding at that location), generates an entirely fake lower bill (which was covered by the food in the original image), and adds a dark malar line on the throat that Grasshopper Sparrows do not have. This illustrates the danger of using AI to enhance photos—it can fundamentally change the natural history, morphology, and plumage of the subject in the original photo. The original photo is great, and exactly what we would want added to the Macaulay Library without passing it through AI.
While still rare, we have seen a few instances of fully generative AI photos meant to deceive others or to make a nicer representation of what the user saw. Never, under any circumstance, submit a fully generative AI photo to eBird. Intentional falsification is an egregious violation of our Community Guidelines and can result in your eBird account being permanently set to Not Public, removing all your checklists and media from public and scientific uses.
Example 3. Fully AI generated. An AI-generated photo was uploaded to the Macaulay Library as an Elfin-woods Warbler ( Setophaga angelae ). This photo is not only unnaturally sharp with a photo-perfect pose, but the AI also made the bird look more like a Black-and-white Warbler ( Mniotilta varia ) than an Elfin-woods Warbler. These sorts of fake, fully-generated photos are never acceptable in the Macaulay Library.
