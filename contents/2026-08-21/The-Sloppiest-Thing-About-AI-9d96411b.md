## Original Extract

Calling every AI-assisted work slop is not discernment. It is a refusal to judge the work itself.

The Sloppiest Thing About AI — oxblog
OXBLOG … because I forget simple little things too soon …
There is a fashionable new way to avoid thinking. Notice an em dash, a tidy
list or a sentence with suspiciously polished edges. Announce that AI was
involved. Dismiss the whole thing.
The post may be correct. The product may work. The patch may fix the bug. None
of that matters once the detector in someone’s head has beeped. The work has
been placed in the AI bucket, the bucket has been labelled slop , and
judgment can stop before it becomes tiring.
AI did not create this habit. It merely brought it to the surface. Plenty of
people have always cared more about the signals surrounding an idea than the
idea itself. Tell them a person they admire said something and they lean
forward. Tell them the same words came from someone they despise and they stop
listening. Now authorship by a machine provides an even cheaper excuse.
Where something came from can matter. It can expose a conflict of interest,
establish authority or tell us who is responsible. What it cannot do is spare
us from examining the thing itself.
Is the claim true? Is the reasoning sound? Does the product solve the stated
problem? Can the contributor explain and defend the patch? Those questions
measure substance. “Was AI involved?” does not answer any of them.
A beautifully written falsehood remains false. An awkwardly written insight
remains an insight. A human can produce empty, derivative nonsense with great
confidence; a machine can help express a useful idea with unusual clarity.
The origin does not reverse the result.
Anyone who changes an opinion merely because they discovered who — or what —
formed the sentence was not evaluating the sentence. They were evaluating a
social cue and mistaking the sensation for thought.
AI slop is real. Cheap generation has made it possible to flood every inbox,
issue tracker and publishing system with plausible-looking material that no
one cared enough to verify. The review cost is real too: ten seconds of
generation can impose ten minutes of work on someone else.
Reject that behaviour without hesitation. Reject fabricated claims,
repetition, empty summaries, untested patches, evasive authors and submissions
whose creators cannot answer basic questions about them. Rate-limit the flood.
Close the gate when there are not enough people to operate it. None of this
requires pretending that every use of AI produces the same result.
Calling all AI-assisted work “slop” is itself the sloppiest possible act of
classification: putting unlike things into one bucket because teasing out the
differences requires effort. It is intellectual laziness dressed as taste.
Sometimes it is worse — a performance of discernment by people unwilling to
do any discerning.
Projects, publications and communities that ban AI-generated contributions
usually present the ban as a defence of quality. It is an admission that they
have chosen not to measure quality.
Good rules describe the failure they are designed to prevent. Require
evidence. Require tests. Require disclosure where it is relevant. Require the
submitter to understand, revise and take responsibility for every word or line
they send. Reject duplication and mass submission. These rules work whether
the offending material came from a model, a careless human or a human using a
model carelessly.
A provenance ban does the opposite. It rejects honest contributors while
rewarding anyone willing to conceal their tools. It removes people who have
something useful to contribute but lack the time, confidence or writing skill
to package it in the approved manner. And it guarantees that some good work
will be discarded for no defect present in the work.
This does not mean a maintainer must accept every contribution. No volunteer
owes the internet an unlimited review queue. But a thousand hours of unpaid
work buys gratitude, not infallibility. Scarcity of maintainer attention is a
sound reason to control volume. It is not evidence that an entire class of
tools is incapable of producing value.
Design for the world that exists
Generated material is not a passing current one can nobly swim against. It is
a collapse in the cost of producing words, images and code. That change will
not be reversed by a rule in a contribution guide.
The serious response is to build better filters around the properties that
matter: truth, originality, usefulness, accountability and cost imposed on
others. The unserious response is to prohibit the new tool and congratulate
oneself for preserving standards. One adapts the system to reality. The other
tries to preserve the appearance of the old world while the ground moves
underneath it.
This is what makes blanket bans so contemptible. They take the easiest
available action — say no to a category — and leave the difficult work of
preserving quality undone. They protect a familiar surface at the expense of
the substance they claim to defend. Then, inevitably, the communities using
better judgment receive contributions that the purists trained themselves not
to see.
Authorship does not remove responsibility
Using AI is not an exemption from standards. If you submit the work, you own
it. You own its errors, omissions, invented citations, insecure code and
tedious prose. “The model did it” is no more acceptable than “my editor did
it” or “my compiler allowed it.” A tool can assist production; it cannot
inherit responsibility.
That is the boundary worth enforcing. Ban unaccountable work. Ban abuse. Ban
deception where disclosure is required. Do not ban useful work because the
person behind it used leverage.
Every sentence in this article was generated by AI from my argument,
direction and requested tone.
If that fact created an urge to dismiss what you had just read, examine the
urge. Nothing in the argument changed. No claim became less true. No sentence
became less coherent. You merely learned something about the process and felt
tempted to substitute it for an assessment of the result.
Judge the work. Demand evidence. Demand accountability. Reject rubbish without
apology. Just do not confuse recognising a tool with exercising judgment.
