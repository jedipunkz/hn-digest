How supermarkets are using AI to watch you in the aisles
Analysis: Shops have always used retail surveillance, but AI has supercharged it in so many ways, including some you might not even notice
AI is creeping into every corner of our lives, and shopping is no exception. Walk into almost any shop today and you are being watched, whether you notice it or not. Cameras track your route around the aisles, smart shelves note what you pick up and put back, hidden sensors map exactly where you linger, and increasingly, facial recognition spots your arrival before you have even grabbed a basket.
Yet none of this is brand new. Shops have used CCTV (security camera systems) and security guards for decades to stop theft and keep everyone safe. What has changed is the scale, speed, and predictive power AI brings to it. There are also surveillance systems you would never spot: 3D-sensors quietly mapping your movements, thermal imaging, even mannequins fitted with hidden cameras .
Sensor-camera systems, like the tech behind Amazon Go stores , can track what you take off a shelf and bill you on your way out, no till required. Predictive AI can track how long you linger in a zone, or read your body language, and flag you as a "risk" before anything even happens. Meanwhile, your loyalty app quietly builds a profile of who you are and what you will buy next, drawing on your in-store visits, online browsing, and social media activity.

Surveillance is best understood not as one system, but as an assemblage : a shifting mix of technology, people and space, working together without any single company fully in control. AI did not invent retail surveillance, but it has supercharged it, changing how you experience and respond to it in ways you might not even notice. Here is how you have ended up part of the retail surveillance system yourself.
The first is changing your own behaviour. When you know, or even just suspect, you are being watched, you adjust how you act. This is not new: it was the logic behind Jeremy Bentham's 18th century prison design , built so inmates could never be sure if they were being observed, so they behaved as if they always were.
My research with retail customers found a similar mix of feelings: cameras can feel intrusive, even "creepy," yet also reassuring, especially when shopping alone late at night. That is the trade-off most of us have quietly accepted: surveillance is constraining, but it also feels protective. AI-powered facial recognition takes this further. If a system can recognise you the second you walk in, greet you by name, or flag you as a "loyal customer" worth a discount, your identity is known, tracked, and quietly graded before you have picked up a single item.

You end up watching everyone else too
The second phenomenon flips the usual surveillance story on its head. You are not just watched, you watch back. During the pandemic , this played out in supermarkets everywhere, with customers policing other shoppers over mask-wearing and social distancing, and even keeping an eye on staff over hygiene rules. Some staff found themselves fielding complaints from customers demanding they enforce rules they had no power to enforce.
Beyond the pandemic, this habit continues through apps that let you scan products, check prices, or call out bad service online. Bodycams worn by staff, meant to protect workers and customers alike, add another layer, and customers increasingly film alleged bad behaviour and post it online. You have become, in effect, a part-time surveillance agent, whether you signed up for the job or not.
You're seduced into handing it over
The third phenomenon is probably the one you will recognise instantly: seduction. You are not just monitored, you're coaxed into handing over your data for rewards, discounts, free Wi-Fi, or a faster checkout. Most customers happily answer a barrage of questions from a cashier just to save a few euros - a small, immediate perk that outweighs the personal information given up.

Therefore, you are no longer disciplined into compliance through fear, you are seduced into playing along because it feels good and it is convenient. Biometric payment and facial recognition checkouts are the newest version of this old trade: hand over your face, your fingerprint, or your habits, and shopping gets a little cheaper and smoother.
Your data builds a "digital you"
Finally, all this adds up to building a digital version of you based on your purchase history, loyalty activity, app use, and in-store movements - information that retailers later use to sort and target you. AI supercharges this, cross-referencing in-store visits, online shopping, social media and location data into a digital identity that can follow you from browsing on your phone to a targeted ad on your social media, to a personalised offer on the shop floor.
Some people manage this actively, deleting loyalty accounts once they stop using a shop. Others shrug it off as the price of convenience, comparing it to social media; you don't pay for Facebook because your data is the cost of using it, and your loyalty card works exactly the same way.
