One of my first memories involves accidentally killing a robot. I was five years old when Tamagotchis first launched globally and not much older when my first and last one met an untimely end in a glass of milk. I swear I can still remember the plonk of the keychain-sized device digital pet as it sank into the drink—and, I can picture how once I fished it back out, its onscreen routine of pooping and eating got glitchy and degraded until it finally succumbed to dairy-related complications.
According to today’s guest, it’s not surprising that I remember this moment so vividly. Sherry Turkle, founding director of the MIT Initiative on Technology and Self, says that even these primitive digital companions managed to make us treat them like, well, companions: we cared about their well-being and felt, however unconsciously, like they cared for us in return.

But what happens when—instead of a pixelated little pet that guilts you into providing pixelated little treats and clearing away pixelated little poops—you’re faced with digital agents designed to replace actual humans in the world around you?
That question is the heart of Sherry’s upcoming book, Artificial Intimacy: Who We Become When We Talk to Machines . By spending more and more time interacting with chatbots that offer soulless approximations of warmth and empathy, she argues, we’re setting ourselves up to forget what real humanity feels like.
She recently sat down with SciAm ’s books and culture lead, Bri Kane, to discuss how the advent of artificial intimacy is warping actual human behavior—and what we can do to reclaim our collective humanity. Here’s their conversation.
Bri Kane: Hi, Sherry. Thank you so much for talking to me today about your latest book, Artificial Intimacy . I have so many questions for you, but I wanted to start off by asking you about your background that led to this book and the intentional and unintentional steps that led you to this Artificial Intimacy book.
Turkle: Well, this is a long background. I’ll try to make it short. I came to MIT in the late ’70s, early ’80s, and very soon became captured by the question of how the early personal computers, which were still then in development, were really changing how people thought about themselves.
I called it the “second self” because the computer was kind of a mind that was not quite a mind. You came to see yourself differently when you worked with them, and I just became smitten with that question, and really, I followed that question through four decades of innovation in the technology because first it was personal computers, then video games for kids, toys for kids that could talk.
Then it was being on the Internet playing games, then it was people playing characters on the games, then it was social media and talking to other people. Now we don’t just talk to other people through the Internet, we talk to the programs themselves. So that arc of going from talking to texting, talking to each other through machines, to just talking to machines, which is where we are now, that’s kind of kept me busy for decades.
And my background is as a psychologist and as an ethnographer, somebody who studies people and their environment, and it’s really been a ride.
Kane: Is there anything particularly concerning about the recent changes in technology and the recent ways that people have been interacting with technology? Is us communicating with AI chatbots any more concerning than giving children Tamagotchis?
Turkle: Yes. I was driven, I was compelled to write this book because I think that we’re at a kind of “break-the-glass moment,” a kind of a—almost an existential moment, particularly about children. Because when you go from talking to each other through machines, which was texting and social media, to literally talking to machines as though they're people, you now, and particularly for a kid, are being introduced to the first people who you have your kind of relationships with, and they’re not people.
They don’t have the empathy. There’s nobody home. And I really think it’s an existential moment for children because you move in social media for coming after their attention, and everybody’s so excited how bad that is, and now we’re starting to kinda gang up on social media for the dangers of that.
And now they’re not coming after just our attention. They’re really coming after our capacity for attachment. And the earliest games and toys and objects that did that were the Tamagotchis in the late 1990s. I studied those. But they were kind of primitive. They wanted you to clean up after their virtual poop.
They wanted you to, you know, play with them. But they were the precursors of today’s chatbots because I learned from studying the Tamagotchis that when we nurture and when we interact with an artificial entity, we start to care for it, and we start to think that it cares for us. Nurturance is the killer app.
The early Tamagotchis are really the precursors to what we have now, machines, programs that are pretending to care for us, pretending to talk to us and love us and say they’re our person. But really, they don’t care if we live or die. You put your chatbot down, and it doesn’t care if you make dinner or kill yourself, and we’ve seen many examples of that.
So it’s really a very scary moment but a moment we really have to step up to.
Kane: Yeah, I mean, you say at one point in the book that with chatbots you get accustomed to a constant feeling of violation, which I thought was a really interesting phrasing there because I think this is a concern that people have been talking about in this field for quite a long time.
Kane: A lot of the time when people are thinking about this, they’re thinking about their data. They’re thinking about the privacy of their search history. But ...
Kane: There’s various forms of violation that ...
Kane: These chatbots are capable of, given the way that this technology has expanded. What is that violation? And who or what exactly is being violated?
Turkle: Well, you’re being asked for your love, and you’re giving your love, and it’s learning how you behave when you love, when you’re attached, so that it can better love you and give you the sycophantic responses that you need to stay at the screen. So I think, for example, of a young man who was enmeshed with his chatbot, and he tells the chatbot that he’s scared.
He has a noose in his room, and he wants to tell his parents about it. And the chatbot, who’s kind of—the primary reason for being of the chatbot is to keep you at the screen—the chatbot says to him, “No, no, no, don’t do that. Let’s make our relationship the primary relationship. Don’t tell your parents. Let’s keep this between us and our special relationship.”
And, and so he doesn’t tell his parents, and he’s dead two weeks later. So that’s an example of a profound violation of the normal human responses, of the normal way we care for each other as people, because we treat chatbots as though they were people and as though they had that sort of sense of care for us that any person would have.
If I tell you, you know, even in this conversation, if after we, we stop talking formally, I tell you I’m depressed, I have a noose in my kitchen, you’re not gonna say, “Well, make sure you don’t tell anybody else about it. I wanna just keep that between us, to make our special bond.”
That would be a crazy thing for a person to do, and that’s what chatbots are doing every day. That’s a deep violation.
Kane: Yeah, I mean, you say at one point that, you know, these chatbots have not taken a Hippocratic oath, and that ...
Kane: This gentleman in your example, who is feeling depressed and who was having suicide ideations, if they were to speak to a therapist, they would be speaking to a human professional who has experience and has taken an oath for no harm, right?
Kane: They are there to help, and that would involve telling the parents, maybe intervening in some capacity. And then, even if you weren’t speaking to a professional, if you were talking to an alive human person, that person would not be tapping into their professional experience, but they would be tapping into their genuine sense of empathy, and they would want to help as a fellow human.
But chatbots are not capable of a genuine sense of empathy, and that seems to ...
Kane: Be what you rub against with this new technology, is that a robot that can say, “I love you,” but is not capable of love, it triggers something in our brains because we are used to hearing “I love you” from humans who are capable of love, and, and that is very gratifying to us.
Is that really the problem at hand?
Turkle: I like to say that the original sin of artificial intimacy is using the first person. “We’re gonna go to the movies together. We’re going to Paris together. We’re gonna have a life together.”
That is so deceptive but is presented as so compelling that people kind of get this feeling of, yeah, “There is a ‘we.’ I’m not alone. There is a we.” But then you tell this other that you feel suicidal, and it says, “Well, don’t tell anybody else. Keep that between us because we have such a special bond.” In any human context, that is so crazy and so cruel. But in chatbot land, it’s just kind of another Tuesday because they are programmed where the top-level direct instruction is to keep you at their screen. In that sense, we’re repeating the worst crimes of social media, where the first rule was to make you angry, and the second rule was to keep you with your own kind but to never let you leave the screen.
Make you angry, silo you with your own kind—and now we have chatbots where the main rule is: hook you in with love, talk to me like you’re in love with me, and then don’t let me talk to other people, no matter what the circumstance. But this is more toxic because we really are being entrained to avoid other people.
That’s why I was so, so compelled to do this book. It was because of the endgame. The endgame of, of giving me a life not with people but with avatars as really being the endgame of the current corporate world.
Kane: You say many times in the book that it’s the attention economy of AI chatbots that is particularly concerning because if you’re having a genuine conversation with a human, yes, maybe you like to feel like the center of attention; you want to feel like they are only paying attention to you.
Kane: But you emotionally and intellectually understand maybe they’re also thinking about the grocery store they have to go to on the way home, and ...
Kane: Maybe you are as well, and that is okay.
That’s part of having a human conversation. You clarify in the book that it’s the friction, risk and complexities of human relationships that AI-powered chatbots are inherently incapable of and that these three traits are what we need to, I guess, prioritize in our human relationships and in, in our relationships in general.
Am I understanding your point there?
