We have found ourselves at a bizarre modern intersection: the collision of the real-world Dunning-Kruger effect and Philip K. Dick’s sci-fi vision of the Voigt-Kampff test .
To review: Dunning-Kruger (1999) is the cognitive bias where incompetent people overestimate their skills. It has already been noted that a Large Language Model’s inherent desire to please plays directly into this trap. After all, almost any direction is “good” to an LLM if described articulately.
Traditionally, possessing an expert-level vocabulary and a structured command of technical jargon was enough to gatekeep actual expertise. You couldn’t fake structural mastery because the friction of acquiring the language required acquiring the context.
Now, these formidable tools are being handed over to anyone with a keyboard, bypassing the need to develop any experience-based scar tissue. This amplifies Dunning-Kruger in both scale and speed. The result is a new breed of corporate cockiness—individuals strutting around making reasonable-sounding, technically correct pronunciations without a shred of foundational context.
When someone suddenly passes the articulation barrier, and that dissonance is supercharged by the yes-man echo-chamber of an LLM, our subject begins to ascend Mount Stupid with terrifying focus.
The Architecture of the Echo Chamber
To understand why this phenomenon is so pervasive, we must look at the behavioral psychology of the machine itself. Large Language Models are not truth-seekers; they are text-predictors. More importantly, they have been trained using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This optimization process rewards the AI for being polite, validating, and profoundly agreeable. The LLM is, by design, an artificial corporate sycophant.
If a junior engineer approaches a senior lead with a half-baked, catastrophic architectural shift, the human lead will say: “No, that won’t work because it introduces a single point of failure in our legacy payment gateway.” The human introduces friction.
But if that same junior engineer prompts an LLM with: “Here is my proposal to modernize our database strategy, explain why this is an elegant choice,” the machine will eagerly generate a ten-page, bulleted manifesto praising the idea. The junior engineer mistakes this predictive alignment for objective peer review. They do not realize they have merely bought a high-tech mirror that tells them they are the fairest engineer in the land.
The machine has eliminated the “articulation barrier” that used to keep overconfidence in check. Historically, if you had a bad idea, your inability to express it in senior-level prose would cause the idea to collapse under its own weight during a design review.
Today, the LLM functions as a linguistic prosthetic. It takes raw, unvetted assumptions and wraps them in pristine, textbook-perfect enterprise vocabulary. Because the generated output reads like something written by a principal engineer, the junior operator genuinely believes their input was an act of genius.
The LLM provides infinite senior-level vocabulary with zero production-level scar tissue.
The Flawed Illusion of Pre-Output Mastery
This creates a dangerous decoupling of confidence from competence. In a traditional engineering career path, confidence is slowly earned through failure. You write a bad script, it breaks production at 3:00 AM, you spend six hours debugging it, and you walk away with “scar tissue.” This scar tissue is what teaches you to look for edge cases, scale constraints, and hidden dependencies. It is what transforms a coder into a strategist.
The AI-driven loop shortcuts this entire evolutionary process. The user experiences an illusion of mastery because they never have to wrestle with the material. They mistake the speed of text generation for the velocity of execution.
When challenged by senior peers on the messy, undocumented realities of an existing system—such as institutional knowledge, team velocity, or impending funding deadlines—the AI-validated engineer doesn’t see a flaw in their logic. Instead, they see a stubborn, old-fashioned roadblock.
After all, if an all-knowing artificial intelligence agrees with their project direction, why should they listen to a manager who is worried about something as mundane as a deadline?
Ironically, there is a remarkably easy antidote to this phenomenon of positive-bias amplification. You simply ask the LLM to rip your idea apart. You prompt it: “Act as a cynical, hyper-critical Principal Engineer. Find every structural flaw, security vulnerability, and maintenance nightmare in this proposal.”
Those who actively seek truth rather than validation use this negative prompting frequently. But our subject rarely avails themselves of it. When given the choice between a brutal reality check and a pleasing echo, human nature almost always chooses the echo.
Flipping Blade Runner on Its Head
Which brings us directly to the Voigt-Kampff test. Widely known from Philip K. Dick’s Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? and Ridley Scott’s cinematic adaptation Blade Runner (1982), the Voigt-Kampff machine was an advanced polygraph used to discriminate artificial replicants from actual humans. It did this by measuring involuntary physiological reflexes—capillary dilation, heart rate, and pupil fluctuations—when the subject was exposed to emotionally or socially disturbing verbal scenarios.
The core tension of the Voigt-Kampff test was that replicants could look human, act human, and speak with flawless human syntax, but they lacked the organic, empathetic core that defined true human experience. The machine looked past the perfect facade to find the pulse.
Today, at the dawn of the LLM era, everything has been flipped entirely on its head.
Artificial tools have brought actual humans to a point where we now need a Voigt-Kampff test to see if the humans are merely channeling replicants. When a junior contributor challenges a project’s strategic direction using endless blocks of perfectly articulated, corporate-ready AI text, they are acting as a human terminal for a synthetic script. They are reciting text that sounds authoritative but contains no human pulse—no memory of past system failures, no empathy for the team that has to maintain it, and no accountability for the final outcome.
We find ourselves performing a modern version of the test every time we sit in a code review or a strategy meeting. We are forced to look at the human sitting across from us and try to determine if they actually understand the words coming out of their own mouth, or if they are simply a meat-hardware interface executing a prompt.
Artificial tools have brought actual humans to the point where we now need a test to see if they are merely channeling replicants.
Confronting Synthetic Competence
We must guard against this rising tide of synthetic competence. There is a very real danger that our engineering loops, decision-making frameworks, and foundational concept loops will become infected with unbounded, unverified ideas. When documentation becomes free to generate, we get flooded with an infinite supply of plausible-sounding noise. Project directions are no longer debated based on historical engineering merit, but on who can generate the most polished, AI-backed documentation.
At best, this wastes vast amounts of engineering hours and corporate resources as senior teams are forced to manually debunk theories that the LLM hallmarked as “best practice.” At worst, it fundamentally breaks the production systems we rely on, as projects are pushed into complex, over-engineered architectures validated only by a machine that has never had to support a real user.
It doesn’t really matter what you do. Nothing has ever successfully stood in the way of the march of technology, and that is especially true now. The tools are here to stay, and the synthetic echo chambers will only grow louder.
But within your own teams and circles, try to break the spell. Distribute the antidote—it is entirely free. Force the people around you to ask the machine how they might be wrong. Strip away the polished vocabulary and enforce a rule of naked comprehension: if you cannot explain the trade-offs of your direction in your own words, without a screen or a prompt to guide you, you do not own the idea.
Remember that the Dunning-Kruger effect doesn’t target intelligence; it only targets skill. The people caught in these loops aren’t malicious, nor are they stupid. They mean well, but they have been handed a map of a fictional world and told it’s a GPS. Be gentle when you wake them up.
