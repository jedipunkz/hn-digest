What does your prompt actually cost?

We know what you're thinking... Isn't this site AI generated?
Yes, honestly. Building and refining this page took an estimated 40 rounds of AI coding: debugging layouts, rewriting copy, checking figures against research. Run that through this site's own Reasoning Agent numbers and it comes out to roughly 45 phone charges of electricity. That's not a confession. It's the point. The tool used to build a site about AI's hidden footprint has one too, and making that visible, even for something this small, is exactly why The Invisible Cloud exists.
What it looks like in real life
Vivid ways to picture the invisible.
Generate around 44 images and you've spent as much electricity as charging a phone from empty. A single busy afternoon of logo concepts can rack that up without you noticing.
Ten rounds of back-and-forth with a Reasoning Agent, the kind of editing session that goes in circles, burns enough power to keep a bulb lit for the rest of the evening.
A one-minute AI film in Reasoning mode can carry roughly the same tailpipe emissions as driving to the local shop and back. All for footage nobody drove anywhere to get.
About thirty reasoning-mode prompts, roughly a long brainstorming session, evaporate a shot glass of fresh water to keep the servers from overheating.
How to shrink your AI footprint
Six small habits that actually add up.
A vague prompt gets a vague answer, which usually means a second attempt to fix it. The more detail you give upfront, tone, format, the exact ask, the more likely the first result is the one you actually keep.
Reasoning Agent mode exists for genuinely hard problems, not for fixing a typo or rephrasing a sentence. Save the heavier model for the moments that actually need the extra thinking.
If a result is close but not quite right, ask for the specific change rather than regenerating the whole thing. A full re-run spends the entire cost again; a targeted edit spends a fraction of it.
Treat images and video as material
Generate on purpose, the way you'd use any other resource you can see running out. A dozen quick throwaway variations can cost roughly as much as one carefully planned image.
The tenth variation rarely beats the third. Give yourself a rough cap before you start generating, the same way you'd budget paint, film, or any other physical material.
A good output doesn't expire. Save it, reuse it across drafts and projects, and only generate a fresh one when the old one genuinely won't do.
Understanding AI's environmental impact
Why a calculator like this exists in the first place.
Search interest in green AI has climbed fast, and for good reason. Every prompt, image, and video you generate draws power from somewhere, and that AI energy consumption adds up across billions of requests worldwide every day. The Invisible Cloud translates that abstract AI power consumption into things you can actually picture: a phone charge, a lit bulb, a mile of driving.
The environmental impact of AI isn't limited to electricity either. Running large models depends on data center energy usage that also demands real water for cooling, and that electricity is tied to the same grid behind a country's wider carbon footprint of AI. As generative tools scale, questions about AI sustainability and AI's climate change impact are no longer a niche concern. They're becoming part of how people think about the tools they use every day.
This site doesn't ask anyone to stop using AI. It asks a smaller question: what if the cost were visible? Enter your own habits above, choose Standard or Reasoning Agent mode, and see what your own AI energy consumption actually adds up to, sourced from published research rather than guesswork.
The scale here is worth sitting with. The global AI market is projected to grow from roughly $189 billion in 2023 to nearly $5 trillion by 2033, a 25-fold increase in a decade, according to a 2026 report on AI's environmental cost from the United Nations University's Institute for Water, Environment and Health (UNU-INWEH), led by Professor Kaveh Madani. Generative AI alone made up over a fifth of the global AI market in 2026 and is projected to reach 40% by 2030. Global AI expenditure is projected to exceed $2.5 trillion in 2026, with corporate AI investment topping $580 billion in 2025.
What it costs to train a frontier model
Training happens once, but at enormous scale. GPT-4's training run is estimated to have used 50 to 70 GWh of electricity and produced around 25,000 tonnes of CO2e, work that would take roughly 420,000 tree seedlings a decade to offset, about the number of trees across 105 Hyde Parks. Its water footprint came to about 600 million litres, enough to fill 237 Olympic-sized pools. Projections for GPT-5-class models put training electricity closer to 100 GWh, with a water footprint near 1 billion litres. None of that touches actually using the model afterwards. It's the bill before the first prompt is ever typed.
Inference: the part nobody sees
Training gets the headlines, but per the same UNU-INWEH report, the ongoing inference phase, generating responses for billions of everyday interactions, accounts for an estimated 80 to 90% of AI's total energy use. ChatGPT alone is estimated to process around 2.5 billion prompts a day. A typical AI text query is roughly 200 times more energy-intensive than a simple spam filter, and image and video generation sit well above that again. That's the exact gap The Invisible Cloud is built to close: not the training run nobody sees, but the everyday habit that quietly adds up.
Data centers, at a planetary scale
Zoom out further and data centers become infrastructure in their own right. Global data centers consumed an estimated 448 TWh of electricity in 2025, enough to rank 11th in the world if data centers were their own country, with a carbon footprint of around 189 million tonnes of CO2e. By 2030, that could exceed 945 TWh, close to 3% of projected global electricity use, as AI's own share of data center power climbs from roughly 20% to 40%. The report also flags a less-discussed cost: the hardware itself, projecting up to 2.5 million metric tons of e-waste a year by 2030, equivalent to discarding around 250 Eiffel Towers annually.
Every energy figure here traces to a named peer-reviewed study or first-party disclosure, not a blended estimate: Jegham et al. (2025) for text and coding, Oviedo et al. (Joule, 2026) for reasoning-model text, Iyengar et al. (2025) for image generation, and Jegham, Gamazaychikov & Luccioni (2026) for video. Several of these disagree sharply even within their own papers. Reasoning-tier text is contested by more than 2× across studies; reasoning-tier coding differs by more than 5× between sources; the same image-generation paper spans configurations by more than 2×; and 8-second video clips range across the field by more than 10× depending on model. The Standard/Reasoning Agent split follows each paper's own lightweight-versus-high-compute model pairing, not an independent multiplier; for image and video, "Reasoning" means the high-compute tier rather than literal reasoning. On the conversion side, water use assumes Microsoft's global fleet average of 0.27 mL evaporated per Wh, a figure Google's own 2023/24 disclosure puts more than 4× higher for AI-serving data centers; miles, straws and tree-days ride a CO2-equivalence bridge (IEA global grid intensity × a per-target standard) rather than a direct energy comparison. Treat every number as a transparent, sourced reference point, not a universal constant: actual costs vary by model, hardware, and grid.
