By Tejas Kumar , AI Engineer at IBM
This morning Elon Musk posted 3 lines on X : “No more AI”, “SI”, “It’s better”. By lunchtime in Berlin it had 2.8 million views and he’d agreed to rename SpaceXAI to SpaceXSI . He’s following an executive order that President Trump signed on 29 September, which tells every US federal agency to say “Super Intelligence” instead of “Artificial Intelligence” and to “not acknowledge the usage of ‘Artificial Intelligence’ and ‘AI’ in any applicable setting”. When reporters asked him about it, the President explained : “We’re using the word super because the other word is a fake word.”
The other word is artificial . It comes from the Latin artificium (a thing made with skill, from ars , art, and facere , to make), so artificial intelligence means intelligence that people made. Fake? We literally made it. We just don’t want to say so anymore.
People have been doing this for a very long time. This post is about why we do it, and about the difference between super and superior , which we keep blurring and which matters a lot more than the name.
AI is changing to Super Intelligence (SI) because a US executive order signed on 29 September 2026, Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence , tells federal agencies to use “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in place of “Artificial Intelligence” and “AI” in everything they publish. Its first section argues that today’s systems “do much more than imitate or automate discrete aspects of human intelligence”, so the old name is too small for them. Nobody outside the government has to follow it, but the word was already fashionable in Silicon Valley: Meta named its AI lab Meta Superintelligence Labs in June 2025 and Microsoft started a superintelligence team that November.
The definition stayed exactly where it was. Section 3 says “Super Intelligence” means whatever federal law already called “artificial intelligence”, which is insane … it’s literally just a rename: “a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments.” Same machine, same words, human-defined objectives and all.
There’s a man in the Bible who did exactly this. His excuse might be the funniest line in Scripture.
In Exodus 32 , Moses has been up the mountain with God for 40 days and the people waiting at the bottom are done waiting. They go to his brother Aaron and say “ Come, make us gods who will go before us ”. Aaron has them take off their gold earrings (their wives’ and kids’ too), melts them down and “ with an engraving tool he fashioned it into a molten calf ”. By the end of that same verse the people are shouting that these are their gods, “who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!” A calf they just watched him make out of their own jewelry is getting the credit for the plagues and the Red Sea .
Then Moses comes down the mountain and asks Aaron what on earth happened, so Aaron explains:
So I said to them, ‘Whoever has gold, let him take it off,’ and they gave it to me. And when I threw it into the fire, out came this calf!
Out came this calf. Bro, he had the engraving tool in his hand 20 verses earlier. He made the thing himself!! By the time anyone asked him about it, it had walked out of the fire on its own.
That’s what a rename does: artificial remembers the engraving tool and super says it came out of the fire.
The Adoration of the Golden Calf by Nicolas Poussin , 1633 to 1634, in the National Gallery in London. Moses is the small figure in the top left.
Look at what the calf was made of, too: the people’s own earrings. A language model is made the same way. It learns from text people wrote (books, code, forum threads, Wikipedia , the comments under a recipe) and gives it back to us recast, so when it dazzles us, a lot of what’s dazzling us is us.
John Calvin , the French reformer, looked at how quickly people went back to idols after the flood (“before many years elapse, men are forging gods at will”) and wrote this in his Institutes of the Christian Religion in 1559:
Hence we may infer, that the human mind is, so to speak, a perpetual forge of idols.
A little further down he explains how the forge works: “The mind, in this way, conceives the idol, and the hand gives it birth.”
You’ve probably heard this as “the human heart is an idol factory”, which is how it usually gets quoted in sermons. The word Calvin wrote was ingenium (a person’s wit, their talent, their cleverness), which is where we get the word ingenuity . It’s also where we get engine and engineer . I’m an AI Engineer at IBM, so Calvin’s word for the idol factory is in my job title. Dang.
The executive order uses the same word. Its first section says these systems “amplify human ingenuity”, which is true: I’ve spent the last 4 years building things that do exactly that. Then it promises “limitless opportunities”. The White House fact sheet goes further , with “the limitless promise it offers the American people”. The Bible keeps limitless for God: “ Great is our Lord, and mighty in power; His understanding has no limit. ” An understanding with no limit is what a lot of people picture when they hear superintelligence. The Psalms already gave that title to somebody.
Alan Jacobs wrote the computer version of Calvin’s line in The New Atlantis back in 2017 : “The computer is, as Alan Turing theorized when he first imagined it, the universal machine; it is therefore the universal idol-fabricating device.” His worry was the feed, which manufactures new things for us to want faster than we can get bored of the old ones. This week the device itself got promoted.
I’m not writing this from outside the factory, by the way. In 2020 I wrote about myself that “I sometimes mansplain (to my shame!) because I want people to think I’m smart” ( in this post ). Smart is what I’ve always wanted people to see in me. So a machine with intelligence in its name, and now super in front of it, is aimed right at the part of my heart Calvin was writing about.
The rename came from Washington. The same week, it turned out the people who build these models have been asking a stranger question.
On 29 September, The New York Times published an investigation by its national religion correspondent, Elizabeth Dias , into how Anthropic , the company that makes Claude , has spent months “shuttling in dozens of religious scholars from across the world, papering them with nondisclosure agreements”. It opens on a night in April when an Anthropic leader took a group of them to “a high-end tasting menu restaurant in San Francisco”. All day, the co-founder Chris Olah “had worked to convince his guests that A.I. models could display human behavior and even expressions that resemble feelings like anger and love.” Over dinner, the rabbi sitting next to him noticed that “Anthropic’s leaders were talking about Claude as if it were not mere software.” Back in April, The Washington Post had covered an earlier summit with Christian leaders under the headline “ Can AI be a ‘child of God’? ”
To be fair to Olah, he told the Times plainly that he doesn’t know (“we don’t know if A.I. models are conscious. I don’t know. I’m genuinely uncertain”) and I believe him. His team’s whole job is working out why models like Claude act the way they do. An engineer who says “I don’t know” about the thing he studies every day is being more honest than most of us manage to be about anything. The Times also reports that part of the point was borrowing the world’s moral traditions to train Claude to behave well, which I think is a good instinct.
4 days after the Times story, Sam Altman , who runs OpenAI , posted this:
I am very uncomfortable about people trying to ascribe religious force or a surrender of human judgment to AI models, and think it is a real safety issue.
He didn’t name anyone, but I agree with him. You can even measure the surrender of human judgment: the consultants further down who leaned on a model for a task it couldn’t do got it right less often than the ones working alone. Then again, in 2023 he told the Financial Times that products like ChatGPT are “ channels into our one single product, which is intelligence, magic intelligence in the sky ”. Wild!!
The question is pointed the wrong way, though. In the Bible, being a child of God is a gift, given to people: “ to all who did receive Him, to those who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God ”. The very next verse says what kind of children those are:
children born not of blood, nor of the desire or will of man, but born of God.
Claude was born of the will of man, very literally. A company full of brilliant people willed it into being, out of our words. John rules out exactly that.

A thing that makes the day happen, that ripens every crop you’ll ever eat, that you physically can’t look at, that was there before your grandfather and will be there after your grandchildren. By every measure an ancient farmer could take, the sun was superhuman. Babylon called it Shamash , the god of justice who sees everything. Egypt called it Ra .
So Genesis 1 does something kinda sneaky. On day 4, God makes “ two great lights: the greater light to rule the day and the lesser light to rule the night ”. The Hebrew word for sun is shemesh (almost the same word as Shamash ), so Genesis just doesn’t say it. It calls the sun a light, gives it a job, and makes it 2 days before it makes people.

Backs to God, inside God’s own house, faces toward the brightest thing they could see.

A week before the order, the President ran a poll asking his followers whether they preferred “SUPER INTELLIGENCE” or “SUPERIOR INTELLIGENCE”. Super won. Superior is what a lot of us hear anyway, so let’s be precise about both words.
Super is Latin for “above” or “beyond”. In AI research, superintelligence has a precise meaning, from the philosopher Nick Bostrom ’s 2014 book Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies : “any intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest”. That’s a claim about performance, on nearly everything.
