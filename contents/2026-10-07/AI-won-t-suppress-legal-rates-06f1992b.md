“Top lawyers have for a long time been compensated on the foundation of associates billing for long hours. The ability to complete tasks more quickly marks a fundamental altering of the revenue foundation for these mega firms. Their compensation model is now extraordinarily unstable”.
-Eric Grossman, Morgan Stanley’s general counsel. [ 1 ]
Demand for lawyers’ time grew 4.2%, against a normal 1.5%. [ 2 ] About 90% of legal spending still goes through hourly arrangements, [ 3 ] and nearly 60% of in-house lawyers have seen no noticeable savings from their firms’ use of AI. [ 4 ]
Realised rates, a year earlier +9%
Demand. More work arrived than AI removed, much of it on the AI build-out itself. [ 2 ] , [ 8 ]
Review. “It should take you about the same amount of time to review a first draft of something as it would take you to draft it.” AI is “like a junior associate that I can’t ever fully trust”.
More work per matter. Diligence that once sampled the 10 largest contracts now covers 100, for the same hours.
Other people’s AI. “Obviously that’s another hour on the clock if you’re asking us to review the AI output,” said Addleshaw Goddard’s head of innovation. [ 9 ] One US lawyer billed 12 hours for work that “should have taken 3-4 hours MAX” after a client rewrote the lawyer’s drafts with AI. Defending an AI-assisted pro se employment claim costs “about 10% to 15% more”. [ 10 ]
Habit. “Others are quietly billing the same hours they would have before and pocketing the margin. Nobody wants to talk about that second one but it’s happening.”
What firms do with the savings
One Big Law transactional lawyer said their group is pushed to give fixed quotes on deals that were always hourly, “explicitly so we can use AI, fire juniors and keep (or likely increase) PEP”. The tools are not cheap: Kirkland & Ellis is spending $500 million on its own AI platform, [ 11 ] and Harvey and Legora “are just now starting to try to push the token usage model to firms”. One top-ten New York firm is “attaching an AI component to billable matters in our time entry systems to track when AI was used in work product”. Firms cannot even agree on learning time: Ropes & Gray lets first-years put about 400 hours towards AI, while Polsinelli’s chair says “if you need motivation to learn how to use AI in your career right now, we probably have other things that we should be concerned about with you”. [ 12 ] , [ 13 ]
Some forbid AI outright. A consulting partner saw the same contradiction in their industry: clients demand “lower fees from fewer billable hours ‘because AI productivity’ but none of them want deliverables created by AI”. In-house teams expect the hourly share of their external spend to fall from 72% to 44% within three years. [ 14 ] One general counsel put the firms’ problem in a sentence: “Every efficiency gain AI delivers results in a lower bill for me. That makes AI investment economically irrational for the firm.” [ 15 ] Not every client is chasing price, though. “I hate surprises, okay? My clients hate surprises. My finance team hates surprises,” said JLL’s Seth Gastwirth. [ 16 ]
Under ABA Formal Opinion 512, a lawyer billing by the hour may bill only the time actually spent; a tool’s cost is overhead unless the client agrees otherwise. [ 17 ] Virginia goes further for fixed fees: “It is not per se unreasonable for a lawyer to charge the same non-hourly fee for work done with the assistance of AI as work done without the use of AI.” [ 18 ] California’s 2026 guidance lets matter-specific AI costs be passed on only if the fee agreement discloses them, at cost. [ 19 ] One federal judge has already imagined hourly fees challenged as “unreasonable, because all these tasks that were done by a lawyer could have been done in 30 seconds by an AI tool.” [ 20 ]
“They’re going to start that equation at the end and work backwards. Here’s what we’re making now, let’s create a billing structure that ensures we’re still making that amount.”
“Virtually every lawyer ever worked for did the opposite of padding bills; rather, they ate time wholesale to keep clients happy.” (a retired NYC Big Law partner)”
“The billable hour will be safe at least until an in-house lawyer can go before his Board of Directors … and tell them that he used Claude instead of Prestigious Firm X without worrying about getting fired.”
“It does not mean we are going to sit around twiddling our thumbs … it means we are going to have the capacity to take on more matters.”
“Since these are flat fee matters, this cuts 4 hours worth of research into 1. More money in my pocket.”
AI speeds up drafting and review, but it does not predict an undisclosed issue, an aggressive counterparty or an unexpected ruling, and those are what generally cause matters to run long (as lawyers charging fixed fees are well aware). Between the two, a firm can alternatively split a matter into small fixed fees, one per stage. [ 21 ]
Under hourly billing, the saving belongs to the client; under a fixed fee, the firm may keep it.
So far, firms have kept it, through higher rates and more work.
The biggest clients are now writing it into bids and billing guidelines.
