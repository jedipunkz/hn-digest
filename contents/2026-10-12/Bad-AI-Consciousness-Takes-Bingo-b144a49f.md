To my great surprise, the AI consciousness debate is getting HOT right now. You have to understand how strange this is for me. I’ve been working on consciousness for most of my adult life, and AI consciousness for over a decade. For most of that time, AI consciousness was a respectable but fairly kooky debate in which most of the common examples came from science fiction. But now it’s everywhere! Newspapers, TV, even papal encyclicals . Which of my niche interests is next? Should I expect congressional hearings on Baldur’s Gate 3 Honour Mode strategies?
As fun as this has been, it’s also given rise to a positive cavalcade of terrible takes, including from some people I otherwise think of as smart and thoughtful, which has led to me dropping some tweets that, if not quite acerbic, could only be described as uncharacteristically arch.
To be clear, I don’t think that I have all the answers here, or even most of them. There are plenty of people on all sides of the debate I respect, and while I skew towards “ moderate liberalism ” (AI consciousness is possible, current models are probably not conscious, but we shouldn’t be too complacent about that), I have big error bars in all directions.
But if people are going to weigh in on these issues - as I absolutely think they can and should - they should at least do some basic homework or show a little bit of epistemic humility, and ideally both, because right now I’m experiencing physical pain multiple times a day scrolling Twitter from seeing hot takes that are unreflective, uninformed, and often very crude (in the full sense of that term).
With that in mind, I decided to put on the yoke of necessity and do something about it. Here, inspired by Rob Bensinger’s sublime Bad Alignment Takes Bingo , is the Bad AI Consciousness Takes bingo (I won’t name names, but several Local Area Posters have already won).
I would stress that this list is far from exhaustive, and although it focuses on the anti-AI consciousness side here, there are bad arguments for AI consciousness too (eg, the direct inference from intelligence to consciousness, or accepting multiple realisability as a datum without question). The main difference is that almost everyone on the pro- side I’ve seen so far is saying things like “this is a really important issue and we should take it seriously”, whereas a disproportionate number of people on the anti-side (including some big names and big accounts) are adamant the topic is dumb and stupid and their opponents are obviously wrong. However, should the pro-side start engaging in similar epistemic misbehaviour, rest assured I have another bingo card in me.
Finally, I’d also really emphasise that many of the lines below could be absolutely fine as conclusions of a wider argument or theory. For example, there are some reasonable arguments connecting life and consciousness; I’m not convinced by them, but I have great respect for some of their advocates. The problem is when people assert those conclusions as argument-terminating assumptions.
Anyway, here’s the bingo and the breakdown, have fun and don’t take it too seriously.
Master rebuttal. Everything can be described in lots of ways, without excluding other ways. Humans are clouds of atoms, biochemical complexes, social primates, and doctors, lawyers, and parents, and all of those are mutually compatible.
"It's just programmed by humans." LLMs are not programmed in the sense relevant here; it’s more instructive to say they’re grown . Engineers choose the data, architecture and training objectives, but the ultimate features of the model emerge in complex and often unexpected ways (cf Lovelace’s objection in Turing 1950 ). If being shaped by an outside process ruled out consciousness, natural selection and your parents would have some explaining to do.
"It's just matrix multiplication." And you’re just sodium and potassium ions traversing membranes. Every interesting physical system is "just" something at implementational levels of description (see Marr 1982). The question is what other capacities emerge from these lower level traits. (Also, it’s not even technically correct; LLMs interleave matrix multiplication with non-linear operations, without which the whole network would just collapse into a single matrix multiplication).
"It's just predicting the next word." Next-word prediction is the training objective for base models, but that doesn’t tell you HOW the system is achieving the training objective. Prediction is also a deep cognitive capability; one of the leading theories of human cognition characterises us as predictive engines . Oh, and it’s also untrue that LLMs exclusively predict the next word — post-training adds other objectives besides prediction, and LLMs have been found planning rhymes a line ahead , not just "one word at a time".
"It's just copying human behaviour." LLMs are anthropomimetic , to be sure — they resemble us in terms of behaviour. But that doesn’t mean their behaviour doesn’t have some of the same underlying mechanisms; an understudy may learn by copying a dancer’s steps, but at the end she’s really dancing. Whether the mechanisms are the same is an open question, and "it's copying" restates it without answering it.
"It's just playing a character." This is a more interesting objection, as LLMs do seem to have personas . But it’s not clear whether these are more like an actor playing a role or a human switching between social personas in different settings. Even if it’s the former, actors are conscious. And recent interpretability work treats the network as an author simulating the Assistant rather than being it, which means the thing that might feel and the thing that acts needn’t be the same. The author could be the moral patient and the character the moral agent.
Master rebuttal. No-one knows what consciousness is or what it requires, and people have suggested a wide array of ingredients over the years. All of these takes propose something LLMs lack and assume consciousness needs it. Some of those assumptions may turn out right, but as slogans they’re just presupposing the theory they need to argue for.
"It's not alive." Bacteria and fungi are alive and almost certainly not conscious, so life is at best a necessary condition. There are some serious theories linking consciousness more or less closely to life through processes like metabolism, homeostasis or self-maintenance, and they deserve engagement. But you need to make the effort to actually embed your claim in these frameworks!
"It doesn't have a body." Our bodies do very little while we’re dreaming, yet we still have rich conscious experiences. People with locked-in syndrome have almost no bodily agency but full conscious lives. Embodiment theories are again real contenders, but you need to argue for the claim that consciousness requires embodiment rather than assuming it (we also have various forms of embodied LLM already, of course).
"It doesn't have feelings." This is the precise point at issue. Just because you say it loudly and/or angrily doesn’t make it true. See also proof by assertion .
"It doesn't have free will." Plenty of philosophers and neuroscientists think humans lack free will (Robert Sapolsky had a recent popular book about it ). They vary on what this means for things like responsibility, but none of them concludes it means we’re unconscious. Consciousness and free will are different properties.
"It's frozen until you prompt it." Consumer LLMs remain passive until prompted because of specific design choices, but there are lots of ways of giving LLMs the ability to run continuously and autonomously without user input (see eg OpenClaw ). More broadly, while this may be relevant for determining the m temporal structure of different kinds consciousness, you need an argument if you think only certain temporal structures create consciousness in the first place.
"A simulation of a hurricane doesn't get anyone wet." But a simulation of a calculator does arithmetic, and digital money has value. Some properties survive simulation and some don't. If functionalism is true, consciousness is more like money, defined by its causal role. You may disagree — but again, you have to give an argument rather than just restating your position.
Master rebuttal. Analogies between two things only work if they share relevant features. This is why people laugh at the famous “ You wouldn’t steal a car …” advert against piracy, because there are a number of important differences between conducting a carjacking vs a teenager downloading Nothing_Else_Matters(2).ogg. Many of these arguments are similarly poor analogies.
"Do you think your iPhone is conscious?" Panpsychists and some pancomputationalists actually do, but setting them aside, most people don’t. But most people also don't think sea sponges or bacteria are conscious. Clearly there are other factors besides “is it biological or artificial” that are relevant here, most obviously complexity (The Microsoft Word variant is worse, since a document is a static record and nothing in it computes anything.)
"It's like thinking there's a tiny band inside the radio." Radios work because bands make music in a studio and it gets broadcast and radios pick it up. LLMs have no studio equivalent. Their outputs are produced by the computation happening inside the system, so if anything the model is the band. (Whether the band feels anything is the question.)
"It can't even count the r's in strawberry." This is years out of date, so immediately indicates a ‘familiarity issue’ on the part of the person making it. There’s also a straightforward reason LLMs struggled with this, namely that they read text in chunks called tokens rather than letters. More to the point, capability and consciousness come apart. Toddlers and cats can't count the r's in strawberry either.

"We don't even know what consciousness is." True! It’s the hardest problem in science. But surely that should be a reason for open-mindedness and epistemic humility! We still don’t know which animals are conscious. And as a result it seems at least conceivable that we might build consciousness without knowing it. Of course, it‘s fine to use this line if you’d like to remain agnostic, but you can’t use it to license a firm one-sided verdict against AI consciousness.
"There's no evidence it's conscious." It depends on what you mean by evidence. Direct evidence, no, but you have no direct evidence that anyone but you is conscious. Instead we rely on indirect cues — other people are made like me and act like me, so they’re almost certainly conscious. Animals are made like me but most of them don’t act much like me.
