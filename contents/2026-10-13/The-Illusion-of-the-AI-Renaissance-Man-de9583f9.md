The Illusion of the AI Renaissance Man — Jack W. Smith Skip to content Jack W. Smith Creative Work Blog Contact ↗ October 12, 2026 The Illusion of the AI Renaissance Man
Back in the day, I used to make YouTube videos talking about anime (yes, cringe as hard as you want).
I had to source the clips, cut them, edit the videos, write the script, record my voiceover, render the video, realize I left something out, fix it, re-render the video, and so on.
To be honest, I kind of loathed video editing. Writing the script and thinking about what I wanted to say was the fun part for me. For some of my friends at the time it was the opposite; they loved focusing on the visuals, but sitting down to write a script seemed bothersome.
Today, we live in a world with AI models. I could hand off the video editing to them, or at minimum the arduous task of selecting relevant clips, cutting them to ~5-8 seconds in length (to avoid copyright problems), and stitching them together.
In a way then, some would argue that AI can turn you into some kind of renaissance man. You can do the work of a video editor, script writer, researcher, and also be your own social media manager/marketer at the same time. Right?
But I was doing all of this myself anyway before LLMs even existed in their current form. I already was the renaissance man, bro.
I’m not arguing about the capabilities of AI. I’ve already gone through the five stages of grief and have accepted the fact that these tools are, at minimum, useful for productivity.
In fact, they’re so good at a wide range of tasks that they can quite easily do all of the busywork that you don’t want to do. AI doing the work for you doesn’t really make you a renaissance man, it makes you more of a manager or director. In the long term I think this could breed specialists.
In my YouTube experience, what AI would’ve allowed me to do in reality is become more of a specialist. I’d be entirely focused on writing the script and spending more time honing it, as opposed to learning video editing or sound engineering.
But learning that broad range of skills is what allowed me to find work in the first place. I’m not sure how that changes today with AI.
This push towards speciality is happening in my real work, too. When I write documentation, I no longer carve out hours to learn how to tweak outputs in the web framework I’m using. AI does that, so I get to focus on the documentation itself.
For blog posts, I’m spending much less time scouring Google for sources and charts and statistics. I use AI to write up a research report, check the sources are reliable, and then include them in my own writing.
All of this is to say that I get to focus on my craft (writing words). I can do more, and I’m certainly more productive because of AI. But that’s because it’s doing all of the stuff which would make me a true generalist.
I’ve been operating under the assumption that people, you, me, will still want to keep a craft. I don’t want to outsource writing too, but it would be very easy to do, even though AI writing still needs human guidance (for now).
The question that’s been on my mind for a while now is:
What do you choose to do when AI can already do it?
Productivity, Personal Growth, and P(doom)
People in the AI scene love to talk about P(doom): the probability that AI will wipe out all of humanity. I personally think that the biggest threat (right now, at least) is deciding to outsource everything to AI.
I would consider myself one of the fortunate ones. I grew up reading books and learned how to program before LLMs came into existence. The experience for newcomers now must be something kind of unimaginable.
There’s a tool out there that can do everything you can do, but better. This puts them in a very precarious position: they are pushed to go full LLM and focus on speed, so they don’t fall behind their peers.
I’m not sure how much you can learn doing things this way; I can’t really speak for it. While it’s true that the former might be better for your brain, it’s going to be worse for productivity.
All I can say is that I think it’s a bad idea to outsource everything .
Humans are Natural Generalists
As people use AI to pick up the slack elsewhere, I think it’s preferable to make sure you don’t make it pick up the slack everywhere. You should use the time and effort saved meaningfully.
AI is described as having “spiky” intelligence right now because it’s especially good at math and code. My bet is that we will need to fit ourselves in around the “spiky” intelligence that LLMs seem to have today.
It’s weird. I think on one hand, AI will push us to the pinnacle of specialism which can be a good thing, because we can hone our focus on a single thing more than ever.
Yet, outside the scope of labour, humans are naturally generalists. We cross-pollinate across topics, and have different interests. These can be extremely important for scientific breakthroughs or unique pieces of art. By rejecting this, maybe we’ll lose something in the long run.
I think there is probably a counterargument here focused on getting AI to teach you things, instead of doing the work for you. If AI becomes good enough at this, then I think you can become a sort of renaissance man.
Honestly, I’m not sure. I think my thesis is still changing. AI has made me think a lot about where I want to spend my time. I still want to be as good at writing and marketing as I can be. Outside of work, it’s a bit more tricky.
