What 10 providers disclose about what they charge, what 32 models actually cost across four declared workloads, and five notes from measured usage. Every number on this page is a snapshot of 2026-10-13 and does not move: later editions are new pages, not edits to this one.
Scored providers cannot sponsor this report, this site, or any edition of it. No provider was given advance sight of the scores beyond their own right-of-reply packet. Rubric 1.0 , frozen 2026-08-27 — the RFC was published before any provider was scored. Full dataset (JSON, CC BY 4.0) .
1 — Provider Cost Transparency Index
Ten providers, 25 binary criteria each, 250 cells. A criterion is MET only on primary evidence from the provider's own published material; where our reviewer and verifier read the same page and disagreed, the cell resolves to the stricter reading and says so. Every non-MET cell was re-checked against the live page on 2026-09-13 before publication.
The weakest block is billing / invoice quality : 17 of 50 . Providers document what they are going to charge considerably better than what they charged. Billing predictability scores / ; the invoice itself scores 17 / 50 . The gap is the finding: the document you reconcile against is the least transparent artefact these companies produce.
Two of those invoice failures are not inherited conditions but documented regressions, declared by the providers themselves . OpenAI's help centre states that for Enterprise customers, from invoices issued on 1 April 2026, detailed API costs are no longer provided on invoices, replaced by an export flow. Google Cloud's documentation states that the detailed usage cost line items were removed from invoices in 2021 and that invoice usage details do not include a column for the SKU price. Both moved detail off the invoice and into a separate surface. We record these as changes rather than absences, because that is what they are.
2 — Effective Workload Cost Index
A price per million tokens is not a bill. This index takes four declared workloads and prices them against all 32 models on the board, using each model's real cache, batch and long-context rates where the provider publishes them. A workload profile is a cost model, not a benchmark: it says what a declared pattern of usage costs, not which model is better. Every assumption is declared. Where a provider publishes no cache-read rate the workload is computed WITHOUT cache, so its cost goes up, not down.
Interactive app: one user, many turns, nothing cached.
Same chat app with a stable system prompt + history served from cache.
Agent run: 6 steps, re-sent context, heavy output. Reasoning not priced.
Offline job: 1M requests/mo, list input, short output, batch pricing.
The spread is the point. Identical declared work, identical month, and the difference between the cheapest and the dearest model on the board runs from 134 x to 275 x. The batch pipeline spreads widest because the discount compounds: the cheap model applies a batch tier on an already low rate, and the dear one has no batch tier to apply. The live workload tool recomputes all of this against today's board, which will have drifted from this page.
Five measurements taken from real usage over the quarter, each published in full at the time. They are here because the quarter's pattern is visible only across them: the gap between a price table and a bill is made of cache, tokenizers and thresholds, none of which appear in the price table.
Every provider was contacted through a published channel and offered 14 days to respond before publication. 1 of 10 replied on the substance: OpenAI , criterion by criterion, with no corrections requested.
Three further providers — AWS, Anthropic and Google — answered with redirects to support channels. We record those as received but not as a position: a pointer to a help desk is not a provider's view of its own score, and presenting it as one would overstate what we were told. The remaining six did not respond within the window. No score on this page was changed by a provider.
