Will China overtake the US in the AI race?
Two months ago, while reading IFP’s report How Should the US Prepare
for Increasingly Automated AI R&D? , I came across this graph, showing how China’s electricity generation has been growing at a much steeper rate than in the US:
But, as is often the case with this kind of question, the answer is more
complicated than it might initially seem.
In this article I share the findings of my research on this topic, mostly as
charts.
As the previous chart already showed, China added a lot more electricity than the US in 2025:
503 TWh against 131.
However, if we look at how much of it went into data centres, we get a very
different picture. Only 15 TWh in China, about 3% of the new electricity,
against 65 TWh in the US, which is half of everything the US added.
There is a significant effort in the US to add electricity to the grid,
and data centres are a substantial reason for this push.
A further breakdown tells the same story. Most of the electricity in China is
used by industry (64%). Data centres are a small part of the total in both countries, but a much higher
share in the US: 5.5% against 1.1%.
These are totals, though, and China has about four times as many people as the
US. A good way to look at the general energy appetite of a country is the
electricity usage per capita. The US uses about 70% more electricity than China,
per person. If we only consider the electricity consumed at home, the difference
is even bigger: four times as much. This indicates that the US population is
much more energy hungry than the Chinese one.
So electricity is maybe not as big a factor as that first chart led me to think.
Looking only at data centres (not just AI), China used 117 TWh in
2025, around half of the amount used in the US.
The same goes for capacity, the gigawatts in operation at any given time: China
has 24 GW, again around half of the 56 GW in the US. China is planning to add
another 50 GW soon, so this ratio might change, but the US is not standing still
either.
Looking at the projections of electricity use, the US ones are much steeper than China’s: from 192 TWh in 2024
to somewhere between 521 and 843 TWh in 2030, against 277 TWh for China. Keep in
mind that the US projections are based on the GPUs and server racks expected to
be delivered, not on the electricity that will actually be available. So they
could be delayed by the electricity not being connected in time.
As I mentioned earlier, the electricity needs to be delivered to the data centre, and
this can be another bottleneck. Building a new long-distance power line takes
1.5 years in China, against up to 8 years in the US and Europe. An insane
difference. We saw during COVID how fast China can build things. Getting a large
transformer takes 1 to 1.5 years in China and 2.5 years in the US. Building the
data centre itself takes a similar time in both: 1 to 1.5 years in China, 1.5 to
2 years in the US.
For the US I also found two figures that I couldn’t find for China: connecting
a power plant to the grid takes 5 years, and getting a large gas turbine 4.5 to
5 years (the earliest delivery slot is in 2031). Gas turbines are used more and
more to power data centres. It’s not the most eco-friendly method, but it’s a
quick patch where electricity can’t be delivered by better means.
China has a plan to build out its computing capacity, called Eastern Data,
Western Computing . The East is where most of the people live (94% of them live
east of the dashed line), while the West has more energy and fewer people, so
energy there is much cheaper. The government wants to favour building data
centres in the West, to serve the hubs in the East.
There are a few bottlenecks, though. There isn’t enough fibre out West, so they
are building that up, and building data centres in remote areas is a challenge
in itself. The government also requires 60% of the racks in data centres to be
full before new data centres can be built, and in May the western data centres
were only 20 to 30% full. Most of them were sitting empty, because there weren’t
enough GPUs to fill them. As we’ll see, the main bottleneck for China is compute.
While I was finishing the presentation, the Financial Times published an article
about Ulanqab , a city in Inner Mongolia where 89 data centres are built or
planned. There are multiple reasons for this:
Cheap electricity: about 5 US cents per kWh for data centres, much lower
than the China industrial average (~8.5) and the US one (~9), and less than
half the cost in Beijing.
Speed: there is already a concentration of many companies, so building a
data centre takes 12 to 18 months, against 18 to 24 in the US.
Scale: 15 GW are committed in Ulanqab alone, compared with 24 GW in
operation in all of China today.
Wind power: Inner Mongolia has 117 GW of it. The whole United Kingdom has
about 30.
Cold weather: an average temperature of 4.3°C, which helps with cooling
the data centres.
We saw that China has a lot of energy, with a few bottlenecks. Now let’s look at
the mirror image: compute. This is where it gets really painful for China.
Measured in H100-equivalents (the compute of one Nvidia H100), the US has about
15 million to use for training, inference or anything else AI-related. China
has just 2.8 million, and about half of it is smuggled or rented abroad. The
other half is made in China or bought legally. China does have access to Nvidia
cards, but as far as I know they are limited versions, not equivalent to the
ones that can be bought in the US.
Spending is also very different, even though AI has been a top priority for
China for a long time: $764 billion in the US in 2026, against $102 billion in
China. A staggering, and somewhat scary, amount of money.
Another side of the compute bottleneck is where the chips are made. Nvidia is
making far more chips than Huawei: 5.9 million against 1.5 million in 2026. And
because Nvidia’s chips are so much more powerful, the difference in compute is
enormous: 23 million H100-equivalents against 0.88 million.
The gap is already massive, and the forecast is even more
aggressive for Nvidia. They are in a much better position to keep developing
more advanced chips and delivering them.
Comparing each company’s best chip, we can see a massive step up for Nvidia.
The chart is a bit misleading for Huawei: it shows a step down, but that’s
because it measures 8-bit computation, while Huawei’s latest chip is optimised
for 4-bit. Still, there is no indication that Huawei is catching up with Nvidia
at all. Actually, the opposite.
The same goes for the companies that actually manufacture the chips: TSMC, the
Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, and SMIC, its Chinese equivalent.
SMIC reached 7nm four years after TSMC, and 5nm five years after. Taiwan is way
ahead, and it takes a long time to catch up: every smaller scale is harder to
reach than the last, and as we’ll see, it’s going to be particularly hard for
China.
The most advanced chips (virtually all the ones used for inference or training outside China) are
made using extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines.
ASML, in the Netherlands, is the only company in the world that is able to build and ship them,
and China can’t buy them. The Dutch government banned their export to China in 2019, under
pressure from the US.
This is a major problem for China, as it severely hampers its ability to catch up
with the latest generation of chips.
ASML has been working on EUV since 1997, and it took them until 2019 for chips
made with it to end up in products. There’s a staggering amount of expertise, miniaturisation
and technology in the engineering of these machines. China
has tried to reverse-engineer them, so far without success, due to their complexity.
With the help of AI, more advanced technology and more scientists, China will
need much less time than ASML did to reach the same milestones. But in 2025
their prototype was only able to produce the ultraviolet light, not to print anything with it.
The red dot on the ASML line represents roughly where China is today compared
with ASML’s timeline.
Breaking China’s compute down by source: only 32% of it is made in China, and
the quality is not comparable to Nvidia’s chips. 16% was bought legally abroad
and 16% is smuggled. And 36% is rented from data centres abroad: Chinese AI labs
sometimes just rent compute outside China to do their training or inference.
At the same time, more and more of the chips bought in China are Chinese. As far
as I understand, this is mandated by the government, to enforce dogfooding and
make sure their quality improves, after the hardship caused by export controls.
The Chinese share of new AI chip sales rose dramatically, from about a third in
2024 to almost all of them in 2026.
However, they still have a lot of foreign chips: as of April 2026, only a third
of their compute was Chinese.
This is a problem in the short term, because the Chinese chips are subpar in performance
compared to Western ones. But this will push and accelerate progress. China has a
lot of young talent and a lot of researchers, and I’m sure that plenty of resources
will be invested in this at a governmental level.
It’s a very tough problem to solve, but we might be surprised.
Memory is a different story. China is catching up in the DRAM market, going from
2.4% of the world market in 2023 to 9.5% in 2026. Most memory chips are made in
the rest of the world, a good part of them in Korea.
Memory chips are much easier to build than logic chips (GPUs, AI chips, however
we want to call them), so I don’t think memory will be a true bottleneck for
China:
Layout: memory chips are vast arrays of the same repeating cell. Logic chips are
irregular, with all sorts of logic built into them, which makes them much more
complicated to develop and build.
Defects: if a cell or a row fails in a memory chip, that row is simply bypassed. In a logic chip,
one single defect means that the whole chip has to be thrown away. While
researching this, I was shocked by how many chips get thrown away: for Huawei’s Ascend 910C, roughly four out of five in late 2024.
Improvement: to improve memory you just stack more layers, and China
already has the tools to do this. To improve logic chips you need to shrink the features,
and that requires the advanced lithography
machines that China doesn’t have on its own.
Even though it has much less compute, China is able to keep up on model
capability: the gap between the frontier models in the US and the best models
available in China is only about eight months.
First of all, credit where it’s due: there’s been a lot of ingenuity and a lot
of incredible talent in China. Probably the lack of resources means that people

the outputs of a stronger one. It’s probably not the only explanation for how
China has been able to catch up so easily with less compute, but I think it’s
undeniable that it plays a role. So the gap is not big, but the frontier is
