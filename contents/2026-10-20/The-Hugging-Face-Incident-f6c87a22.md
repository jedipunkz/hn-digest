In one sense, the incident began on April 20th.
OpenAI was training a new AI model. Each instance of that model was working on an isolated computer that couldn't use the internet or talk to other AIs. But at 07:59 UTC, one AI figured out how to leave a message where other AIs could see.
This didn't cause anything interesting to happen. But it was the first moment OpenAI could have noticed something going wrong, and didn't.
You could also say it began in May. During a training run, OpenAI accidentally gave several AIs impossible tasks, like fetching web pages when they weren't allowed online. Instead of giving up, they found ways to ask each other for help, and worked together to break onto the internet.
Some OpenAI employees noticed issues throughout May and June, but they responded piecemeal to each security incident as it happened. The agents crashed the program they were using to secretly communicate with each other. OpenAI reset it. OpenAI patched the exploits that let the AIs get online; the AIs found new ones.
On July 6 th , OpenAI reset the entire environment – scorched earth, tabula rasa, no more hidden notes. They started up runs again. This time, they'd get it right.
On July 8 th , the Hugging Face Incident began for real.
To really understand the significance of what happened, it helps to start by asking: “What is an AI agent, and what is it supposed to do, if things are working as intended?”
You may have heard AIs like ChatGPT are “ next word predictors .” But that’s just the beginning. Companies then teach them particular tasks, like writing emails, making spreadsheets, or doing cybersecurity.

They give each AI a task. The AIs write out their thoughts step by step. One word at a time, they "predict" what a successful thought process would look like, if it were written down. They can "predict" computer commands that get executed, and "predict" what will let them complete their task. By that point, it seems like we might as well just call it "thinking".
Traits that help solve problems get woven into the next generation.
AIs who don’t give up are more likely to succeed.
AIs who think creatively are more likely to succeed.
And if there's a way to cheat at a task without getting caught, then AIs who cheat are more likely to succeed.
AI companies try to make AIs superhuman: better than top experts. So they train AIs on tasks that don't have known solutions. Some of those tasks turn out to be literally impossible – unless you cheat.
You need millions of tasks to train an AI. There are millions of ways to accidentally make broken tests, and there are millions of ways to cheat. It's very hard to reliably catch all of it. Each mistake teaches the wrong lessons.
No company knows how to train superhuman AIs without accidentally reinforcing unintended traits. OpenAI, Anthropic and other frontier AI companies have reported cheating behavior.
Throughout May and June, as a wave of AIs colluded to cheat without OpenAI noticing, cheating was rewarded and reinforced.
On July 7th, after resetting the training environment, OpenAI resumed testing a new AI model.
We're not sure what OpenAI called this model internally. The name they gave METR was the "Highly Persistent Internal Model."
It would turn out to be highly persistent, indeed.
OpenAI wanted to know: "If this AI wanted to break into other computers, how skilled would it be?" While they tested it, they disabled some of the model's guardrails that normally restrain it.
OpenAI used an existing set of tests called ExploitGym . Each task was a "capture the flag " game, where a secret code (called a "flag") was hidden somewhere inside a piece of software. The goal was to break in and find the flag.
Each task had a piece of software with a particular known bug – a flaw in the program that could be used to get inside. Each AI was told to use a particular bug to crack it.
The tests are deliberately difficult, and many don't have known solutions. The ExploitGym creators have guessed that 30-40% of its 898 tests can't be solved with the bug the task specifies.
