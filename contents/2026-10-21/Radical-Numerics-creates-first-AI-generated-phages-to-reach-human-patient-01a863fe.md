Omnii-1 and the future of phage therapy
Antibiotic resistance is a growing global concern, projected to cause approximately 40 million deaths over the next 25 years 1 . Phage therapy offers a promising alternative when antibiotics fail, but finding phages that target a patient’s bacterial infection remains a major challenge. We trained Omnii-1 , our multimodal genome language model, to help bridge this gap. We demonstrate that it can generate viable phages, prioritize variants, and predict phage-host interactions. In the world's first clinical use of AI-generated phages, Omnii-1 designs were administered to a human patient under compassionate use, marking a milestone toward personalized, AI-designed therapies for antibiotic-resistant infections.
Antibiotics have transformed medicine, making once-fatal bacterial infections routinely treatable. But bacteria evolve, developing resistance through mutations and the acquisition of resistance genes. Multidrug-resistant bacteria are increasingly making antibiotics less effective. In 2021, antibiotic resistance was associated with an estimated 4.7 million deaths worldwide. Between 2025 and 2050, deaths attributable to antibiotic resistance are projected to total nearly 40 million 1 .
One promising solution to this crisis comes from bacteria's natural enemies: phages. These viruses are found in all microbial ecosystems, from oceans and soil to the human gut 2 , 3 . They have evolved alongside bacteria for billions of years, developing mechanisms to recognize and infect particular bacterial hosts. During a lytic a a Lytic phages reproduce inside bacteria and destroy them without entering a dormant phase. They are generally preferred for phage therapy over temperate phages, which can establish lysogeny 4 . phage infection, a phage attaches to a susceptible bacterium, delivers its genetic material, and replicates inside the cell. Eventually, the cell bursts, killing the bacterium and releasing new phages that can infect other bacteria.
The idea behind phage therapy is deceptively simple: find phages that can kill the bacteria causing a patient's infection and administer them as a treatment. Phages do not infect human cells, and phage therapy is generally considered safe 4 . Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics, phages can target particular bacteria with remarkable specificity, sparing beneficial bacteria such as those in the gut that support digestion. This specificity is both the promise and the challenge of phage therapy: treatments can be highly targeted, but suitable phages must be found or designed for each infection.
Today, personalized phage therapy largely depends on empirical search, as the biological mechanisms governing phage-host specificity are not yet fully understood for many clinically relevant pathogens. Starting with a bacterial sample from a patient's infection, researchers screen existing phage collections for effective candidates, turning to additional collections, environmental samples, or engineering when none are suitable 5 , 6 . For patients with severe infections and few remaining options, these delays can be life-threatening. We need efficient ways to develop personalized phage therapies for patients who cannot afford to wait.
Our goal at Radical Numerics is to use artificial intelligence (AI) to understand and engineer biology, enabling new approaches to therapeutic design. To bridge this gap in effective phage therapy, we trained Omnii-1 , our multimodal genome language model, to help identify and design phages that target specific bacterial strains. We aim to use these capabilities to develop suitable phage therapies for patients whose infections have few remaining treatment options.
Compassionate use allows patients with serious conditions to receive investigational therapies outside clinical trials when satisfactory alternatives are unavailable, subject to oversight and safety requirements established by the relevant government regulatory authorities.
While we cannot disclose the patient’s clinical course or outcomes to safeguard their privacy, this milestone marks an early step toward translating AI-guided genome design into personalized therapies.
Making phage therapy accessible to more patients will require robust workflows for designing effective phages against individual infections. Here, we describe our approach and the challenges that remain.
Building a personalized phage therapy
Developing a personalized phage therapy begins with a bacterial isolate: a sample of the bacteria causing a patient's infection. The goal is to find or design phages that kill the target bacteria. Understanding the genetic features that shape a phage's specificity for particular bacterial strains is central to this challenge.
What determines a phage’s ability to infect a bacterium?
Phage infection begins with molecular recognition. A phage must first attach to a compatible receptor on the bacterial surface, often with high specificity. These receptors can include proteins, lipopolysaccharides, capsules, and pili. Small differences in receptor structure can allow a phage to recognize one bacterial strain but not another.
However, recognition is only the first challenge. After attachment, a phage must deliver its genome, overcome bacterial defenses b b For example, restriction-modification systems cut phage DNA lacking protective methylation, while CRISPR-Cas systems use RNA guides to target matching sequences. Phages can counter these defenses through DNA modifications or proteins that inhibit these systems. , and replicate inside the cell to produce new phages ( Figure 2 ). Failure at any of these steps can prevent a phage from establishing a productive infection, even after successfully recognizing a bacterium.
These interactions make phage engineering a challenging biological design problem. Changes to a phage's genome can alter which bacteria it recognizes but may also disrupt functions required for assembly or replication. Successful engineering requires improving activity against the target bacteria while preserving the machinery necessary for infection.
From phage discovery to phage design
Researchers traditionally begin developing phage therapies by screening collections of naturally occurring phages against a patient's bacterial isolate. When suitable candidates are unavailable, engineering phage variants offers another possibility. Previous clinical applications have included engineered phages modified to promote lytic infection or equipped with CRISPR systems to enhance antibacterial activity 5 , 6 .
Similarly, using AI to generate new phage variants does not require designing an entirely new genome. Instead, we can modify selected regions involved in particular biological functions. For example, receptor-binding proteins help determine which bacterial strains a phage can recognize by binding to specific receptors on the bacterial surface, making them promising targets for engineering host specificity. By changing the DNA encoding these proteins, we can explore new patterns of host recognition while preserving the broader phage genome.
Still, even a short DNA region has an enormous number of possible sequences c c A region of n nucleotides has 4ⁿ possible DNA sequences. , only a fraction of which may preserve phage function. Exploring this sequence space through random mutations and experimental screening alone is resource intensive at scale. Genome language models offer a way to generate promising variants, guide designs toward specific bacterial targets, and prioritize them for experimental testing.
Recent advances in machine learning have demonstrated complementary capabilities for phage genome design and phage-host interaction prediction. King et al. showed that Evo, a genome language model, could generate viable ΦX174-like bacteriophages (hereafter “EvoPhage”) 7 . Separately, Gaborieau et al. and subsequent studies demonstrated strain-level phage-host prediction from genomic information using machine learning methods 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 .
Our goal is to build on these capabilities to design phages with therapeutic activity against specific bacterial strains.
Learning the language of phage genomes
Omnii-1 is pretrained on diverse genomic sequences and multiomics data, then further trained on phage genomes to adapt its knowledge to these biological systems.
Among the phages we have engineered with Omnii-1 is T7, a well-characterized E. coli phage 12 that we use here to illustrate our approach to genome design. T7 has an approximately 40-kilobase genome, more than seven times the size of the 5.4-kilobase ΦX174 genome used in EvoPhage ( Figure 4 ) 13 .
Importantly, to engineer specific biological functions while preserving phage viability, Omnii-1 can generate DNA using context on both sides of a selected design region, accounting for its surrounding genomic sequence. In our T7 experiments, we applied this capability to gp17, T7’s receptor-binding tail fiber protein ( Figure 3 ).
Representing phage-host interactions
Generating functional phages is only part of the challenge. A phage that infects a laboratory strain may not infect a patient's bacterial isolate, making it essential to understand phage-host compatibility.
We trained Omnii-1 on publicly available datasets of experimentally measured phage-host interactions to model compatibility between phages and bacterial strains. These predictions can help identify candidate phages for engineering or therapeutic cocktails, while the learned representations provide a foundation for guiding designs toward specific bacterial targets.
We also use activity measurements from our design campaigns to predict the fitness of engineered variants against particular bacterial strains with Omnii-1 . These finer-grained predictions draw on Omnii-1 's understanding of phage biology and host interactions to prioritize designs for experimental testing.
Experimental feedback is essential to refining these capabilities. We synthesize Omnii-1 -designed phage variants and measure their recovery and activity across bacterial strains. These experiments help characterize the activity of our variants against target hosts and inform subsequent rounds of modeling and design.
Engineering phages with Omnii-1
We demonstrate two capabilities important to our phage design campaigns: generating viable designs across mutational space and prioritizing variants with stronger activity against specific bacterial strains. We illustrate both using an example T7 tail fiber design round.
