If you work at a university today, you talk a lot with the dead. I don’t mean that as a metaphor – or, at least, not entirely.
Generative AI trains on people. Its algorithms learn from humans, requiring our quirks and idiosyncrasies to improve. If the models are exposed predominantly to the output from other AIs, they degenerate and, eventually, collapse .
That explains a strange new trend reported by antiquarian booksellers, who told the Guardian of bulk orders for old stock, originating, they thought, from AI companies or their surrogates. The titles were bafflingly esoteric: one bibliophile described a purchase that included a manual on soil mechanics from the 1970s, a book called Born to Thunder: Champions of New Zealand Cycling, a history of the Melbourne suburb of Hawthorn, and a copy of Early Australian Poetry.
But for AI trainers, the subjects don’t necessarily matter. Back in the day, the companies vacuumed up human content from Reddit, Wikipedia and similar sites. With data now prohibitively expensive, remaindered books provide an attractive option for “destructive scanning”, in which machines slice apart bindings to feed on the creativity contained therein.
As an academic, you encounter the process at the opposite end. When you open Speed Grader to confront another bland but competent assignment, suspiciously devoid of grammatical or spelling errors, your heart sinks. You know that, in all probability, the document you’re reading was composed not by your student (whose contribution likely extended no further than dropping the essay question into Claude’s interface and adding “make it sound human”), but by a legion of absent (and, quite probably, deceased) authors, whose forgotten texts enabled an algorithm to stitch together an essay that is at least passable and often reasonably good.
Grading induces a sense of utter futility: what could be less meaningful than offering feedback to dead people?
No one knows the proportion of Australian students reliant on AI for university assessments. One study of 8,000 people found 40% admitting they had cheated via algorithm. But that was in 2024 – aeons ago, given the rapidity of technological improvement.
A 2025 survey by the ARC Centre of Excellence for Automated Decision-Making and Society at RMIT identified students as particularly heavy users of AI. Nearly 80% employed the technology – though the study did not specify what for. Research on American undergraduates conducted in 2024 by UC Berkeley’s Center for Studies in Higher Education suggested two-thirds used AI . The following year, a UK analysis suggested generative AI was “near universal” among British students.
The figures don’t necessarily refer to assessment. My students use AI for all manner of tasks. Many send emails inflected with the slightly old-fashioned ChatGPT or DeepSeek obsequity, so requests for extensions read like missives from 19th century courtiers. Research suggests rates of cheating vary significantly by discipline, with certain fields faring better than others.
Still, if you believe in the integrity of traditional assessment, I have an AI-generated bridge to sell you.
“Why memorise facts or do equations or slog it out over an essay when ChatGPT or Gemini can do it in seconds?” a Macquarie University student identified as “Hayden” asked rhetorically in a recent report by the Australian’s Ros Thomas:
You get everything in the blink of an eye – any equation, any answer, any essay. It’s all being hacked by AI and the sooner people accept that the better. Stop worrying about what’s right and wrong, because everyone’s using it and you’d be dumb not to.
Thomas presses Hayden as to whether he knows any students who complete their assignments without AI. “Nup,” he replies. “Not a single person.”
The issue extends beyond students and beyond assessment. AI use proliferates in higher education because decades of neoliberal reforms have baked into universities a certain way of thinking, entirely compatible with algorithmic models.
To put it another way, the reliance of both students and staff on generative AI results from a broader crisis – a crisis which generative AI, in turn, intensifies. For that reason, AI will, I think, increasingly become a focus for the deep discontent already percolating throughout the sector.
Let’s establish what is at stake.
I teach a subject on the history of journalism. In it, I quote the scholar
Charlie Beckett’s description of how the media first integrated generative AI, not through revolutionary changes, but via tools (transcription software, grammar checkers, and so on) that automated activities most reporters regarded as tedious and inconsequential.
“Artificial intelligence,” Beckett says, “is a collection of ideas, technologies, and techniques that relate to a computer system’s capacity to perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence.”
A definition that stresses the “what” rather than the “how” usefully focuses discussion on pragmatic choices in a pragmatic industry. Yet, in other contexts, it is deeply misleading.
I invite my class to visualise a university in which all students rely on ChatGPT to complete their assignments. They do not find this difficult. I tell them some universities – for example, Adelaide University, the University of Newcastle, Deakin University and RMIT – encourage staff to employ AI tools to accelerate grading, though all insist a human assessor signs off the result.
What would it mean, I ask, if both academics and students relied completely on AI?
In one sense, a university in which both parties outsourced assessment to machines would simply illustrate the process Beckett described, with tasks hitherto reliant on human intelligence instead performed automatically. Yet, in such a scenario, the substitution of technology for brain power would do more than increase efficiency. If university bots graded bots, you would no longer have a university. You’d have a tomb, an academic mausoleum in which digital phantoms murmured emptily.
The scale of the problem in Australian education became apparent in May this year, when the academic Kylie Moore-Gilbert wrote a piece for the Nine papers accusing “all of Australia’s universities” of “committing widespread, industrial-scale fraud”. She described a situation so dire that she considered advising her stepdaughter to think twice about attending university, a place where “kids are now taking on tens of thousands of dollars in debt to have a terrible campus experience while being graded on who can write the best AI prompts”.
Cath Ellis, Western Sydney University’s pro-vice-chancellor for quality and integrity, responded in the same newspapers. She reminded Moore-Gilbert that, for many young people, “university is not an optional extra”, but the “only route through to their chosen profession”. While acknowledging AI posed pedagogical challenges, Ellis insisted universities were not “turning a blind eye”, but were finding ways to encourage students to “value their own thinking”.
Her article culminated in suggestions for Moore-Gilbert’s stepdaughter and other high school students contemplating an academic future. “Don’t cut corners,” the professor urged:
Don’t outsource your thinking, however tempting it may be. […] Real learning has never been easy. That is precisely why it matters. And the graduates who do that hard work are exactly the ones we still need.
The wisdom was rather undercut by revelations that Ellis’ article relied so heavily on AI that the Age and the Sydney Morning Herald removed it from their sites .
A few months later, the same episode played out again, this time in the US. Santiago Schnell, provost of Dartmouth College, wrote for the Washington Post , explaining how educators needed to ensure they were assessing the capacity of students rather than AI-generated work, because
a degree is a public claim as well as a private benefit. Employers, patients, clients and citizens rely on it as evidence that its holder can exercise judgment.
Schnell insisted universities needed new measures to “deter cheating and help professors see what students can do without AI”.
Students might have been forgiven for wondering what professors could do without AI, as the university paper The Dartmouth suggested the provost relied on generated text , not only for the Washington Post article but scores of others, including scholarly work.
I cite these incidents not to single out the individuals involved, but to caution against attributing the universities’ AI problem to the failings of callow undergraduates – or, indeed, merely to problems with assessment. Symptomatically, Western Sydney University responded to the Ellis situation not with an apology, but a justification.
“To write her opinion article,” the university explained,
Ellis uploaded 40,000 words of her own original materials into a Copilot Large Language Model (LLM). The model summarised her extensive base of knowledge, providing prompts. This was the basis of the early drafts, reflecting [Ellis’] own thinking, ideas and opinions built up over more than a decade of dedicated work as a global leader in this field.
The statement concluded by pronouncing “the AI use in this case was appropriate”.
The willingness to defend an article Nine removed for violating editorial policy hints at my broader point: the “outsourcing of thinking” has become, to employ a suitably computational phrase, not a bug but a feature of the neoliberal university.
Managerial language may well be to the information age what the machine and the assembly line were to the industrial. It is mechanised language. Like a machine, it removes the need for thinking: this essential and uniquely human faculty is suspended.
That’s Don Watson, Paul Keating’s former speech writer, in his 2003 book Death Sentence , a polemic against the rhetoric of managerialism. The next year, when Watson compiled a dictionary highlighting the replacement of “living words” with “dead ones”, he sourced many of his examples from the nation’s universities.
The proliferation of “mechanical language” in higher education represents more than a rhetorical failure. It is a linguistic manifestation of the process that, long before ChatGPT had been invented, primed the universities for AI. To understand why Western Sydney genuinely cannot grasp the problem with its pro-vice-chancellor’s intervention we need to look back at the 1980s.
In that decade, John Dawkins , Bob Hawke’s energetic education minister, set about restructuring the university sector on the basis of what was then called “economic rationalism” and we now know as “neoliberalism”. Government funding fell from something like 80% in the 1980s to around 40% , while enrolments grew substantially.

The Dawkins plan promised increased productivity, as the universities implemented managerial strategies familiar from other industries.
