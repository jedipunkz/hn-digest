Note: Current answer, as of October 26, 2026, data through September 2026: not yet across the economy; the clearest sign is weaker employment of young workers in the most exposed quarter of occupations
In the year to September 2026, jobs in the 135 occupations exposed to AI (score 0.5 or more) were 2.8% above the same months of 2021-22, against 3.7% above for the rest, a gap smaller than the survey's margin of error. Before ChatGPT the same exposed occupations had grown faster (+3.5% against -0.8% from 2018-19 to 2021-22). Workers aged 22 to 25 are the exception: in the most exposed quarter of occupations their employment grew 0.1% while young employment elsewhere grew 9.6%. Confidence: medium.
Scale: the 135 occupations scoring 0.5 or more on the exposure scale hold 25% of jobs. All jobs grew 3.3% ( Census CPS , Eloundou et al. ).
Jobs that shrank: 17 exposed occupations fell by more than the noise, among them Customer service reps (-8.7%) , Non-retail sales supervisors (-14.0%) , IT managers (-16.0%) , Programmers (-24.0%) , with 1.68 million fewer jobs together. 20 others rose by more than the noise and added 2.37 million, including Software developers (+18.5%) , Project managers (+30.6%) , Other computer jobs (+24.4%) . Secretaries and general office clerks, which moved by similar numbers in opposite directions, and a catch-all business occupation whose series jumps several-fold, are left out of these examples as likely coding shifts.
Young workers: the household survey gap in the most exposed quarter of occupations matches payroll data showing employment of ages 22 to 25 in the most exposed jobs 19% below less-exposed peers (August 2026), while the whole occupational mix shows no clear AI footprint ( Stanford , Yale Budget Lab ).
Every bubble is an occupation sized by its jobs; hollow bubbles moved by less than the survey can detect. Click one to open its page, or open it full size .

Exposure alone does not decide it. 13% of exposed occupations fell beyond the noise against 7% of low-exposure ones (17 of 135 against 27 of 378, a difference of about 1.7 standard errors), and similar shares rose (15% against 16%). Across occupations, weighted by jobs, the correlation between exposure and employment change is 0.04. In BLS's own categories, "very high" exposure changed +3.6% and "low" -1.6%.
Why medium. The household survey is large for groups and small for single jobs, so most occupations sit within the noise; the exposure scores date from 2023; and no source here isolates cause.
