Over the last couple of years my reading list has been colonized by a single genre: the AI-assisted coding essay. I’ve read excellent specimens in every direction:
converts describing the first time an agent refactored a whole module while they made coffee, half a religious experience;
burned veterans who tried the same trick and lost an afternoon reviewing code that was almost right, which is the most expensive kind of wrong;
and a few serene souls in the middle, calmly weighing pros and cons with an equilibrium I envy and cannot reproduce. 1
I kept postponing my own take on all of this, because everything sensible seemed already written and I’m not enough an expert in this field for my voice to be somehow worth listening to. And then I realized the angle I was looking for had been sitting in a different part of my hard drive the whole time: my Steam library .
I have a small graveyard in my Steam library. It’s the shelf where Early Access games go to receive two patches, a roadmap full of promises, and then eternal silence. The latest entry is a survival crafting game I bought because the trailer looked great and the description used the word “emergent” three times. It emerged, all right: it emerged that the models, the animations and half the interface came from an asset store, and the only original component was the crash reporter.
I think about that graveyard more than I should. I started thinking about it during the post-Covid period, when a friend and I kept hitting the same wall in every early access alpha we tried. That friend is Devorath, a veteran in every sense: we survived Foxhole together, which is the kind of shared trauma that bonds people for life. 2 We had a rotation going:
New World, where I spent more hours in queue than in the game
Myth of Empires, which we don’t talk about . 3
Pax Dei, which looked promising until it didn’t.
Each time the pattern was the same: a trailer that looked like the future of the genre, a feature list that read like a Christmas wish list, and then well… bleah .
The connection to what I do for a living took me a while to see, and now I can’t unsee it. The story of those games is the story of one of the best things that happened to videogames, and at the same time the story of what is happening to our whole industry right now.
Everyone’s a game developer now #
Around the turn of the 2010s, the walls came down. Unity went free, Unreal followed, GameMaker and RPG Maker kept the door open for the 2D crowd, and Steam first opened Greenlight and then removed the gate altogether. A laptop and some patience were suddenly enough to put a game on the largest storefront on the planet, in front of millions of people, with nobody’s permission required.
I want to be precise here, because this part matters: the democratization was wonderful . It gave us one-person labors of love like Stardew Valley, oddities like Undertale, entire genres that exist because somebody could sit down and make them. I refuse to live in a timeline where Vampire Survivors had to pitch itself to a publisher before existing. 4
Making a game required more than tools, though. It required the kind of things you can’t download: dedication, real knowledge of the domain you were simulating, the patience to iterate on level design until it felt right, an ear for dialogue, a sense of pacing, the ability to write characters you’d actually remember a decade later. None of that came in the box with Unity. You either brought it yourself or you didn’t, and the games that still get talked about today are the ones where somebody clearly did.
On top of all of this there’s one last thing that separates the games that survive from the ones that get delisted and forgotten, and it’s the most unmeasurable of the lot: the love for the craft . 5 It’s the quality that makes you boot up a game from 2003 and realize the people who made it cared that the grain prices in Lübeck responded correctly to a bad harvest up the coast. You can’t patch it in, you can’t buy it from a store, and no engine in the world will ever hand it to you for free.
Exhibit A: Patrician III, a Hanseatic trading sim released in 2003 by Ascaron, a small German studio that has been gone for years. Two decades later it still beats the ass of most modern games in its own genre: the economy simulation holds up, the design is tight, and the people who built it were annoyingly skilled at turning good ideas into systems that work. Ascaron went bankrupt in 2009, which in hindsight feels like an omen for this entire post. And yet the same open gate that let Stardew Valley through also let in what the community learned to call asset flips : products assembled from store-bought models and tutorial code, wrapped in a trailer cut to look like the next big thing. The tools multiplied the number of developers by a thousand; they did nothing to multiply the skills. This is where the pattern I keep seeing begins: making became easy, promising started paying better than delivering .
When the promise becomes the product #
There’s an economy of attention that kicks in whenever a craft gets democratized, and videogames spent fifteen years mapping it: if anyone can ship, shipping stops impressing anyone, and attention becomes the thing worth fighting for .
The cheapest way to get attention is to claim something enormous, so that’s what happened: Kickstarter campaigns that promised to redefine genres and delivered demos, trailers labeled “in-engine footage” (an expression I cherish, because it’s technically true and spiritually false), No Man’s Sky shipping in 2016 with a feature list that lived mostly in interviews, Cyberpunk 2077 running on last-gen consoles the way I run on Monday mornings. And then there’s Star Citizen, which sold so many dreams that it now has a war chest on par with the GDP of a micro-nation. 6
I’m aware I picked examples that, for the most part, eventually redeemed themselves over the years. I did it on purpose: I wanted titles anyone who hasn’t spent the last fifteen years in a cave would recognize, rather than some obscure indie that nobody knows. The claims grew more grandiose exactly as the products grew thinner, and I don’t think that’s a coincidence or a moral failing of individual developers. It’s a structural effect: the engine handles memory, physics, netcode and rendering, the tutorial handles the rest. You can operate all of it without any of the fundamentals of computer science, and nothing forces you to notice until the game has to do something the tutorial didn’t cover: save files that corrupt themselves, multiplayer that desyncs when a second player joins, performance that melts as soon as thirty enemies are on screen, a day-one patch that fixes one bug and introduces three.
The trailer, meanwhile, is ready months in advance, because cutting a trailer doesn’t require knowing what a memory leak is. Hype lives in the gap between what you can promise and what you can actually build, and that gap has never been wider.
I watched all of this as a player, mildly annoyed. Then LLMs arrived, and I realized I had seen this film before.
What happened to game development across fifteen years is a subset of what the whole software industry is living through in a handful of them. The barrier to producing code has fallen through the floor: you describe an app, a model writes it, and if your quality bar is “it compiles and the happy path works” , you can have something on the internet by dinner. The software equivalent of the asset flip already exists and thrives: the SaaS launched over a weekend, the landing page with the revenue screenshot, the product that under scrutiny turns out to be a system prompt and a prayer. 7
Claude, build a flawless Web3 AI SaaS platform before my VC meeting in 20 minutes. No placeholders or // TODO tags. Make no mistakes. And the claims inflated accordingly, following the same structural logic, with one escalation: the boldest ones now come from the tool makers themselves . In gaming the promise was “a universe you can live in” . Now it’s artificial general intelligence within a year, give or take, and entire professions, mine included, declared replaceable within six months, on a cadence that repeats roughly every six months. 8 The ratio of claim to deliverable feels familiar because I spent fifteen years calibrating that exact instrument on Steam store pages.
Before it sounds like I’m dunking on everyone in the space, let me be explicit about who I respect in it: the people building actual products on top of frontier models and managing to estimate their expenses correctly. Think about what that job involves. Your main cost of goods is a metered, nondeterministic service, priced per token, whose price list, behavior and availability can shift on somebody else’s quarterly roadmap, while your users do whatever they feel like doing. Making margins work there takes real knowledge and a nerve of steel, and I find the combination fascinating. 9
There’s a premise I’m on board with: in software, the idea matters more than the implementation. A mediocre implementation of a great idea beats a flawless implementation of nothing , and history is full of brilliant codebases that solved problems nobody had.
I know how that sounds in a field where marketing now reigns, where the launch page matters more than the product page and the trailer matters more than the game. I’m not arguing against that reality, I’m arguing past it: hype sells the first copy, the idea is what sells the second million, and nothing sells the hundredth copy if the idea underneath was hollow to begin with.
I arrived at this premise late, though, and guilty as charged: it’s the opposite of what most of us absorb during our studies and the first years of a career, where the implementation is the whole exam and the idea is somebody else’s problem. I spent a non-trivial share of my early years polishing the internals of things nobody needed, and I was proud of the polish.
The twist the LLM era adds is subtle and a bit cruel. Implementation has become cheap, but only its visible part: the first eighty percent, the one that demos well and fits in a screen recording. The rest (edge cases, security, data integrity, the user who does exactly the one thing you didn’t expect) still costs what it always cost, and it still demands the same unfashionable equipment: method, a bit of theory, the patience to read code critically, including code you didn’t write.
This is where the premise gets harder to apply, in a way that took me some time to articulate. The premise still holds, but only when there’s enough signal to tell a great idea from a mediocre one.
An idea, outside your head, only exists through its realization: nobody will ever interact with your concept, they interact with the thing you built. When the implementation is thin enough, the idea doesn’t get a fair trial, because there isn’t enough of it left to judge. The asset flip didn’t fail because assembling other people’s assets was shameful, plenty of decent games do it. It failed because there was no game underneath, and no trailer could fix that.
The pattern wasn’t great idea, botched execution . The pattern was no idea, botched execution , and the trailer was there to make sure you didn’t notice.
I’ve already watched the software version happen: a product that under the landing page had nothing but vibes and a system prompt, vibe-coded into existence in a week, dead within months because the first real users found the holes that one afternoon of code review would have caught.
