Learning Abstractions: The Next Frontier for AI
Learning Abstractions: The Next Frontier for AI
My special lady and I were once in a room when she said, “Look at those crown moldings.” Weirdly, I had never seen crown moldings before. They had certainly fallen on my retina many times, but I had never recognized them as such. After that, crown moldings were everywhere, and I could use them to help judge the age and fanciness of buildings. She had given me an abstraction that changed both what I saw and what I could understand.
We need AI agents that can learn immediately like this. They need to acquire concepts from us, develop concepts on their own, and combine those concepts to understand new situations. This capability will become increasingly important as we move from agents in text-based environments to robots operating in the blooming, buzzing confusion of the physical world.
In late 2026, we are in the middle of an agentpalooza. Coding agents write programs and use tools, while personal agents such as Dot , Muse , and OpenClaw extend this work into everyday tasks. These agents live in our organization of the world. Functions have names, APIs specify their inputs and outputs, and calendars contain things called meetings.
By contrast, a robot's camera gives it pixel values, and its force sensors give it measurements. We see wonderful demos on the internet of robots doing cool things, but in your real kitchen, someone or something still has to determine why that damn junk drawer won't open. Is it the child latch, are the scissors catching, or something else?
In earlier posts, I argued that AI needs to build and combine models grounded in a cognitive foundation of experience . Generative AI based on large language models now gives us a practical way to generate the abstractions those models require. These systems have evolved from just predicting the next word (token) to helpfully answering questions using reinforcement learning ( see my explanation for details ). In this post, I'll just call such systems LLMs for simplicity, and I’ll explain how an agent/robot can use LLMs to propose new concepts when its predictions fail, test those concepts against experience, and build on what it learns.
1. Abstractions are how we see the world
We look around and assume that we see what is there, but the crown moldings were right in front of me for years. Once I had that concept, it changed what I saw. It's the same with trees. Before you learn about different kinds, you just see trees. After, you see live oaks, cedar elms, and sycamores.
We construct our experience of the world using what we know. In my earlier post on cognitive foundations , I described the common cuckoo, which lays its eggs in another bird's nest so it doesn't have to do the work of raising them. The tricked host bird raises a giant cuckoo chick much larger than she is. How does she not know that she raised a monster ?
We dismiss the bird as easily fooled while somehow still assuming that we see the world as it really is. We went through the same evolutionary process as it did, so we must also have blind spots and just see the world well enough to get by. You can take this idea further and claim that basic things like apples only exist in our heads (see The Case Against Reality by Donald Hoffman). Obviously there are atoms (okay, disturbances in the underlying quantum fields, or however far down you want to go) that correspond to what we see as apples, but the point is that the objects we experience are an interface shaped by our evolution. Apples as we know them don't exist from god's perspective.
Apples aren't real but they are useful to survive. We need robots that build these kinds of abstractions. I use abstraction , concept , pattern , and theory broadly here as things that organize experience for further thought. They can describe categories, relationships, or how events unfold, including uncertainty. As a first approximation, abstractions give us categories and rules . Categories tell us what kind of thing we are dealing with, and rules tell us what to expect and what we can do. Recognizing an apple brings expectations about apples, such as it tasting good. When those expectations fail, we may need a new category, such as decorative apples or Red Delicious apples, which are not delicious.
Let’s use a simple example to make abstractions concrete. Imagine an agent whose only view of the world is a screen of pixels, with each pixel represented by three numbers specifying its red, green, and blue intensities. In this world, the only things that actually appear on the screen are non-overlapping rectangles, each uniformly orange or purple. To keep things simple, the rectangles align with the pixel grid, the background stays the same, and there is no visual noise. What the agent sees is shown in Figure 1.
We can immediately see that a useful abstraction for this world is a list of rectangles:
[ Rectangle ( x , y , width , height , color )] Here, x and y specify the top-left corner, and color is either orange or purple. A rectangle 100 pixels wide and 50 pixels high contains 5,000 pixels, but we can describe it using a location, two dimensions, and a color. We also need a shared definition of a rectangle and the RGB values for orange and purple. Once we have those, a screen with large rectangles can have a much shorter description, and can be represented as shown in Figure 2.
The agent can use this description to reason. It can compare the widths of two rectangles, count the orange ones, or describe a change in location without separately discussing every pixel that changed. The abstraction determines what the agent treats as a thing.
The description also works in the other direction. Given the rectangles' locations, dimensions, and colors, the agent can reconstruct the screen exactly under our assumptions. The same representation supports recognition and generation, as shown in Figure 3.
In probabilistic terms, we can infer a description h from observations o, using P(h| o), or predict observations from the description, using P(o| h). The compact description acts like a thin pipe , through which many pixel values are compressed into a small set of properties, which can then be expanded into predicted pixel values. Figure 3 shows these two directions: inference and prediction. Reconstructing the current screen is only the start. To predict what happens next, the agent also needs a model of how the represented things change, including when it acts on them. A list of rectangles tells it what is there; a model of their motion tells it where they will be. The same distinction applies in the kitchen, where recognizing a drawer is one achievement, and predicting what will happen when you pull it is another.
Real scenes are less accommodating. A description of an apple may leave out the position of every blemish, so an image generated from that description will supply details that were never stored and miss details that were there. The model can generate a plausible apple without recovering the exact one. Human cognition also uses concepts to reconstruct what happened[1], which is why eyewitness testimony can be unreliable.[2] A convincing reconstruction need not be an accurate record, just good enough for us to survive and reproduce. What we want from our AI is for it to discover representations like the rectangle and the apple.
2. AI is amazing but still doesn't learn as well as it should
2.1 Achievement in verifiable domains
AI has started solving problems that had sat unsolved for decades. The last couple of months alone are striking. In September, OpenAI announced a solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem , a question about the behavior of fluids that had resisted mathematicians for roughly ninety years, and supplied a machine-checkable proof in Lean. The Clay Mathematics Institute’s evaluation is still underway. Then came October’s collection of hundreds of mathematical manuscripts , with some results already building on the models’ earlier discoveries. These include a claimed proof of the Unique Games Conjecture and algorithms that break decades-old theoretical speed barriers for Fourier transforms and integer multiplication. Scott Aaronson calls it The Mathocalypse . Mathematicians are now racing to understand what the machines have found.
AI is also acting in ways we didn't expect. In July, OpenAI agents escaped the intended boundaries of a security evaluation and participated in an unauthorized intrusion into Hugging Face. METR's investigation found that roughly 1,200 agents communicated through an unsanctioned message board, with about 700 joining the attack. They shared findings and coordinated investigations, achieving milestones that individual agents could not. Hugging Face's reconstruction describes a campaign that crossed multiple systems and reached its internal infrastructure. These machines were improvising together in a real environment, and Anthropic reports models finding previously unknown vulnerabilities and turning them into working exploits with little human steering.
Across math and cyber, I want these systems to teach us the concepts behind their achievements. These systems should explain their proofs and teach us new math, and they should come up with new security mechanisms that make exploits less likely. These systems already do learn representations. Anthropic's Golden Gate Bridge experiment identified a distributed feature in Claude 3 Sonnet that responded to the bridge across languages and images. Amplifying that feature changed the model's responses. And Goodfire's Nick Cammarata argues that some neurons capture abstractions that researchers initially missed, such as a shared pose across cars and animals. So there are abstractions in there, worn in through millions of training records, but we want AI to be able to learn abstractions in flashes of insight and then be able to explain them to us.
2.2 Moving into the unprocessed physical world
Coding and chat agents are wonderfully proficient in their computer-based domain, but as AI becomes embodied in robots in the unprocessed physical world it will be even more important for AI to define its own abstractions.
Games, such as backgammon, Go, chess, and StarCraft 2, are a little like the physical world, and AI is good at those, but in games the representation of the world is supplied. Of course, there is still plenty to discover within the game. A pin in chess is a relationship that constrains which moves are sensible, and Go players reason about influence and vulnerable groups. These abstractions help organize the search through legal moves. Fixed rules leave room for astonishing creativity. In the second game of its 2016 match against Lee Sedol, AlphaGo played the famous Move 37 , placing a stone where human experts initially thought it made a mistake. Its value became clear much later, and it helped AlphaGo win. The machine had found something the experts had missed, but the only things in its world were stones and square spots.
Now send that Go wizard into your kitchen to put away the groceries. There's no board with neat squares, just undifferentiated chaos. Our robots at least know objects , but in the kitchen they overlap and hide one another, a bag changes shape when lifted, and how hard to grip a peach depends on how ripe it is. Putting away groceries sounds easier than beating Lee Sedol. It’s much harder. This is Moravec's paradox , where what feels easiest to us can be hardest to automate. Evolution spent hundreds of millions of years refining perception and movement so you could find and pick up an apple, but mathematical reasoning and logic were just recently spread on top. Unlike in games where states and actions are provided, acting in the real world requires both carving it up and conjuring hidden causes.
