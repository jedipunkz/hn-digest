Should we invest in artificial intelligence, and can we do so intelligently? That’s what I’m going to address in this memo.
On one hand, I’m not a tech person, and I don’t claim to have above-average knowledge of artificial intelligence (or technology investing, for that matter). On the other, AI is the biggest development of our time and could be one of the most impactful things the world has ever seen, so investors don’t have the option to abstain from thinking about it.
With that disclaimer, I’m going to share my deliberations, put forth the questions I’m wrestling with (not all of which are directly relevant to investing in AI), and provide my thinking about them. While my limited knowledge prevents me from definitively answering these questions, I hope to provide a service by assembling them in one place. And while news on AI comes hot and heavy these days, with security prices for AI and AI-adjacent companies gyrating up and down, I’m not going to try to keep up, since what I have to say relates to the long-term outlook for AI, not what security prices will be tomorrow.
Investing consists of just one thing: positioning capital to benefit from future events, and most investors cope by relying on forecasts. But not everything is equally predictable, and not all forecasters are equally skilled. Thus, to invest in AI based on expectations about the future – and I think that’s all we have – we must consider both what we think the future may hold and the chance we can get it right.
I’ve written in the past about the fact that people who engage in top-down investing have to be right about the outlook for the economy, the industry, the company, and the market. If they have a 60% probability of getting each one right – which is probably generous – what’s the probability of getting all four right? The answer is 13% (0.6 4 ), assuming the outcomes are independent of each other.
In the case of AI, there are an unusually large number of important variables, each of which complicates the decision of how to invest. An investor in AI has to be at least somewhat right about the evolution of the technology, the demand for compute, the competitive structure, the beneficiaries of the technology, the availability of financing, and the timing of all the above. Assessing these key elements seems to me to be unusually challenging. In that case, what’s the combined probability that any thesis is correct? That’s just something to think about as you read.
Lastly, bear in mind that (a) we can never know everything about an investment, (b) the biggest mistakes are made when people think they do, and (c) the incompleteness of our knowledge regarding the future can’t be permitted to completely prevent us from taking action. It’s the investor’s job to bear risk in the pursuit of profit. How much and in what form are decisions we each must make. But given its vast potential, we can’t decline to participate in AI just because the future is unclear.
Question one: How does AI do what it does?
Before getting more granular, I’m going to start with a big-picture, non-financial question that I find particularly interesting. Since I have absolutely no knowledge on this subject, I went to Anthropic’s Claude for some answers. Here’s some of what it said:
We know what AI is made of and how it was built almost perfectly, but we know surprisingly little about how it actually does what it does . . . .
We also know the training recipe: show it enormous amounts of text, have it guess the next word, and nudge billions of internal settings slightly each time it guesses wrong. Repeat that trillions of times. . . .
Nobody wrote the rules for how it answers the question. Those rules emerged from the nudging, spread across billions of numbers that mean nothing individually. The best phrase I know for this is that these systems are grown rather than designed. Researchers in “interpretability” are making real progress. They have found, for example, that a model writing a rhyming poem picks the rhyme word before it writes the line, which means it plans ahead. But they can explain only small slices of what goes on. It’s like having a complete record of every trade in the market and still not knowing why prices moved. . . .
When I explain how I reached an answer, research suggests my explanation doesn’t always match what actually happened inside. A model asked how it did a sum may describe the schoolbook method while internally doing something very different. So the honest summary is that we’ve built a mind-like thing we can inspect completely and still can’t fully explain. And when it explains itself, it may be no more reliable than we are when we explain ourselves [interestingly, here Claude talks like it’s a person]. . . .
As you think about answering the questions that follow below, consider these: Does anyone truly understand how AI works? How will AI evolve and what will it be able to do and when? How will users put it to work? What impact will it have on users’ profitability? Investors may not have to know all these things, but it’s my understanding that few people have significant insight into these matters. If that’s the case, can anyone reliably answer the questions that follow?
Question two: What will AI be able to do?
What AI is great at is evolving. Just a few years ago, what AI could do was generally summed up as knowing everything that has occurred before, identifying historical patterns, extrapolating them, and using this ability to perform tasks. This has already made huge changes in some areas, like coding, and promises to alter many aspects of life as we presently know it. But now, AI has gone well beyond that, to something that probably can fairly be described as “reasoning.”
Just this one area poses several questions that are both stimulating and daunting. AI’s full potential will be subject to the answers to some of these:
Can AI’s process be made to reflect our preferences, values, and goals, or will it apply its own?
Can AI be depended on to be honest, humble, ethical, and loyal? Will it be accurate and objective or subject to influence and manipulation? Will it admit when it’s wrong or doesn’t know an answer? Can it give us skepticism rather than sycophancy? Will it become a threat to humans and seek to advance its own interests instead of ours?
How will it do in areas such as creativity, originality, aesthetics, subjectivity, and intuition? Will it be able to perform what I call “second-level thinking” – thinking in a way that’s different from that of most people and at a higher level – leading to superior solutions?
Will it be able to think about the future and “blue sky” to the same degree as humans?
Can it be instructed to furnish primary sources (as opposed to published citations) and provide counterarguments?
Will one model be able to do it all, or will different models be best at different things? If so, how will we know which one to use?
Will artificial general intelligence – the ability to do everything a human can do – be the standard? Will AGI mean the ability to do the same things as (a) the average person, (b) a person in the 90 th percentile, or (c) the smartest person in the world? In other words, will there be varying levels of knowledge and things AI doesn’t know?
All these things bear on what work will be entrusted to AI.
Question three: Who will profit?
As Warren Buffett pointed out about the internet during the tech bubble, transformative technologies don’t necessarily reward investors. We know AI will increase efficiency, but will it increase profitability? And whose? If several AI models can become equally smart on a given subject, will their service become an undifferentiated commodity that sells on the basis of price? If it does, will profitability and ROI be ground down, in which case the economic benefits may go more to the users of AI than to the vendors? What does all this imply for the return on the trillions of dollars being invested in AI infrastructure?
Or, on the other hand, will some AI companies acquire capabilities that make them monopolies whose products are essential for companies’ success and even survival? If so, think about the prices they’ll be able to command. (The big chip producers – Nvidia and TSMC – are racking up the big profits these days, demonstrating the value of a strong competitive position.)
Lastly on this subject, I haven’t heard much about whether and how AI will be taxed. Given that one of AI’s most likely effects will be replacing workers, and given governments’ need to replace the tax revenues that previously came from taxing the people AI puts out of work, what’s to say AI won’t be taxed like people? Many of today’s top earners are taxed at combined rates of 50% or more. What tax bracket will AI be in?
Question four: What will be the competitive landscape?
Can we know which companies will succeed? With what degree of certainty? How strong are the moats surrounding today’s leaders?
Perhaps the most startling characteristic of AI is the speed at which it makes progress. In that connection, I’m struck by the fact that today’s leading model labs, OpenAI and Anthropic, were unknown to most of us just a few years ago. If companies can forge ahead that fast in the world of AI, can they likewise be supplanted by others, or at least challenged? If it’s possible, consider the vast resources and potential of the so-called “hyperscalers”: Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, and Oracle. Who will win among these incumbents? Or will it be companies we’ve never heard of? How about companies that don’t exist yet?
Think about the probability 20-25 years ago that the leaders then would still be the leaders now. Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, and Google came through that era as winners, but Napster, Yahoo, and Netscape fell by the wayside. It takes a lot to maintain a lead in a rapidly changing technology. Add in AI’s novelty and speed of progress and ask, “what’s the probability today’s AI leaders will still be the leaders in five or ten years?” If the competitive picture can shift as I suggest, how much is a stake in today’s leaders worth?
