Karl Deisseroth, Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine: ‘ Advanced AI models could infer the activity of the brain’
The inventor of optogenetics for controlling the brain with light explains how the technique brings closer better treatments for mental illness
The biochemist and psychiatrist Karl Deisseroth, 55, has just won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering how to control the brain with light. Optogenetics makes it possible to control the movement and behavior of laboratory animals by projecting light selectively into different parts of the brain. That makes them spin in circles, alters their ability to socialize or induces symptoms resembling depression. It is a unique window into how the brain works and the origins of mental illness . In one of his latest studies, Deisseroth’s team applied the technique to the heart to speed up its rhythm and showed how that alone produces anxiety and fear-like sensations very similar to those observed in humans. The tool was created by implanting in the brain genes from algae that respond to light, discoveries made by Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel, who share the award with Deisseroth.
This year, an experimental therapy inspired by optogenetics has partially reversed blindness in patients, and others are in development for autism and schizophrenia. In this telephone interview, the Stanford University scientist, who also won the BBVA Foundation Frontiers of Knowledge Award in 2016, discusses his latest experiments, including the creation of transparent human brains.
Question. How far do you think these techniques can go?
Answer. We want to understand the human mind. To do that we’ve started a human clinical research program where we take ideas from optogenetics in mice.
Q. In one of those experiments you induced hallucinations. Why?
A. We did experiments on delivering little puffs of air to the eye, just the kind of thing you might have done at your eye doctor. But we can do this in both mouse and human participants. And they respond very similarly. Both the mouse and the human being get annoyed with these air puffs that an eye doctor might give you. And so we can look with repeated puffs of air while we’re listening in on the neural activity all across the brain in both mouse and human. We’ve been able to see aspects of how that negative state starts and how it spreads. And this will give us ideas for how to intersect it. And we also hope to look at positive emotion as well.
Q. You are also a psychiatrist and see patients. How much hope is there that better treatments for mental illness will appear soon?
A. There are ideas that came from animals that are now in advanced stages of clinical trials in human beings, including for autism and for schizophrenia. Signals look very positive in these trials, and so we hope these will lead to medications for people.
Q. You are also working on treatments for hallucinations caused by Alzheimer’s disease .
A. Yes, it’s called ADP, Alzheimer’s Disease Psychosis [affecting between 30% and 50% of patients]. It’s hallucinations and paranoia that can happen in people with this form of dementia.
Q. Some people say we live in a large social experiment because of artificial intelligence and social networks. Do you think this affects mental health?
A. I am both concerned and hopeful. I do think that there are many problems that arise from misuse of AI, particularly in the realm of mental health. That use of a model that is not closely tied to real human values could end up steering people in the wrong direction, and I’m concerned about that. I’m hopeful though that AI tools will enhance the capabilities of real human beings in helping each other. I do think that as long as a human being who is expressing human values is closely linked to everything that happens with an AI treatment strategy, then it could be helpful. So we have to think very hard about how that’s done to make sure that it doesn’t go in the wrong direction.
Q. You say the brain is the most complex object in the universe. Do you think artificial intelligence will equal or surpass its capacities?
A. I think ultimately, as miraculous as the human brain is, it’s a finite number of neurons, less than 100 billion. And each one of them makes a finite number of connections, you know, about 10,000 connections on average. And in the end, it’s a beautiful, wonderful, mysterious object, but it is an object that has finite properties. And so, although we don’t know yet exactly how, and although it seems impossible, and it seems as though new fundamental ideas will be required, I would still say that, in principle, we should come to understand the human mind and that as a result of that, AI, with our help, may be able to approach some aspects of human mind function. I will also say that the true inner essence of humanity and emotion and subjective sense of being aware — I don’t yet see how AI could achieve that. I don’t yet think that is possible. This is not the computational side of the human brain but more the subjective experience of the human brain. I do not think AI can reach that.
Q. Your technique involves controlling the brain with light. Do you think something similar could ever be done with humans—for example, spying on someone’s brain to know what they are thinking? This is what your colleague Rafael Yuste has warned about .
A. I know Rafael, I think he’s a reasonable person and he’s right to discuss these issues. Without the genetics per se, that one is less of a threat because it’s a somewhat complex technology. It requires to directly apply it to a human being, which is being done. It requires a fair bit of work. You have to deliver a gene to codify, for instance, this light-activated ion channel from algae. And so there has to be a gene therapy and there has be work with light. It’s not something that could act at a distance or without people knowing. But more broadly, I do think that Rafa is correct. With other technologies, advanced AI models could infer the activity of the brain . That’s something we have to be careful of.
Q. In which mental illness do you think you will achieve the best results in the shortest time?
A. One thing that’s been a problem in psychiatry, including for depression and autism and other psychiatric disorders, is we haven’t actually known which cells in the brain are the ones causing the problem. Or which could cause resolution of those problems. Now, with the genetics we do, it’s a very new situation where we can develop medications based on knowing which cells cause or correct the problems. And that is so new and so different and so linked to actual cause and effect mechanisms of diseases and treatment that I will in the long run be able to help people across all of the neuropsychiatric disorders more effectively. That’s not to say current treatments aren’t good, 70% are helped, but 30% of people aren’t helped and I’d like to make that better.
Q. Are you continuing your project to build a transparent human brain ?
A. Yes. We developed a technology to make brains transparent called Clarity, and we’ve developed that thanks to a lot of other methods. One thing we did to make that approach even more powerful is that it lets you see the wiring and the connections of cells. In 2018 we published a next-generation version that lets us not only see the connections between cell and transparent tissue, but also to see the genes expressed by each cell, the little biomolecules, the proteins made by each cells within transparent tissue. And that’s very powerful because that lets you design medications to target those cells. So it’s very complementary to optogenetics. We can see the cells and know if they cause certain symptoms, but then the last question is, how do you design medications to help people based on that knowledge? The way you do that is you have to know which biomolecules, which proteins are in those cells so you can design medications that interact with those proteins. And that’s this next generation version of Clarity that we call StarMap that we published in 2018. Now the goal is to make optogenetics treatments that can be administered with simple pills instead of gene therapy.
Q. At Stanford there is also a center that records patients’ brain activity while electrodes are implanted to treat their conditions. During the procedure they also talk with patients to obtain more precise information about how their brain works.
A. Yes. We run this program at Stanford called the Human Neural Circuitry Program and that indeed is the goal. We hope to make sure that everything we discover is grounded in the human experience.
Q. What is the biggest question you think you can answer?
A. We hope to understand the nature of the internal subjective sense in human beings and to what extent aspects of that are present in mice as well. And for shared or ancestral principles that are experienced across all mammals.
