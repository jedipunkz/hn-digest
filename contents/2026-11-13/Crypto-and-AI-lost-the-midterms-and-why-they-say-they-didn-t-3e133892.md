How crypto and AI lost the midterms, and why they say they didn’t
Crypto and AI super PACs went undefeated in races they were always going to win. In competitive ones, most of their money backed losers.

Crypto and AI super PACs spent $250 million trying to hand-pick the next Congress. But in the races where that money could actually make a difference, most of it backed candidates who lost — though you wouldn’t know it from what the industries are saying.
The most expensive loss came in the Ohio Senate special election to fill the seat vacated by JD Vance. In 2024 , when Sherrod Brown lost his Senate seat, the crypto industry’s flagship Fairshake super PAC took credit almost immediately: “thanks to our efforts, he will be leaving the Senate.” 1 This year, after the industry spent $64 million to keep him from returning, Brown won anyway and Fairshake has had much less to say.
For a race-by-race rundown of the results, see my [noted] post from November 4
The closest thing to a victory lap was a statistic a Fairshake spokesman handed to one industry newsletter. Crypto In America wrote that the network backed winners in 86 of the 93 races it spent in, “a roughly 92% success rate.” 2
That number is much less impressive than it sounds. As I’ve written before , crypto PACs rely on a “back the winners” strategy: spreading relatively small amounts of money across races their candidates were always going to win anyway, to reward loyalty to the industry and pad the PACs’ win rates. It’s the same move Fairshake made after the last election, when it pointed to a claimed 91% win rate in 2024 as proof that “the voters last year were clear”. 3 This time, Fairshake went 74 for 74 in races where its candidate was a clear or likely favorite, but just 10 for 18 in races that were actually competitive. At the end of the day, about half of the $154.5 million it spent had backed candidates who lost. Much of these PACs’ leverage in Congress stems from lawmakers’ fear of their dominance, but that dominance is now significantly diminished.
Crypto’s best results were in the primaries. Industry super PACs spent $48.5 million in competitive primaries and runoffs. $30.8 million of it backed winners including Republicans Barry Moore (AL) and Andy Barr (KY) and Democrats Christian Menefee (TX), Adrian Boafo (MD), and Jasmine Clark (GA). But the PACs notched some major losses, too: $10.3 million in a failed attempt to defeat Democrat Juliana Stratton in the Illinois Senate race, $2.5 million against Democrat La Shawn Ford in Illinois’ 7th District, and $2.1 million against Democrat Oliver Gilbert in Florida’s 24th District [ I103 ]. All three won in November.
As I wrote at the time , it was unclear whether those rebukes previewed a voter backlash against heavy tech spending, or whether they just spent against strong candidates. November brought a clearer answer.
In the general election races that were actually competitive, Fairshake lost three of the four. Those three losses — Republicans Jon Husted in the Ohio Senate race, Bill Huizenga in Michigan’s District 4 , and Bryan Steil in Wisconsin’s District 1 — accounted for $58.6 million of crypto’s $170 million in midterm spending. Its one competitive win was in the Nebraska Senate race, where it spent almost $13.5 million to oppose independent Dan Osborn in his race against incumbent Pete Ricketts, whose seat on the Senate Banking Committee drew crypto’s support.
These three losses are painful for crypto. Brown chaired the Senate Banking Committee until his 2024 defeat, and the industry was euphoric about removing him from one of the most powerful oversight posts. Tyler Winklevoss, co-founder of the crypto exchange Gemini, celebrated the ouster of a “crypto public enemy” and “Elizabeth Warren co-conspirator.” 4 Brown is not expected to retake his Senate Banking chairmanship, which might sound like good news for crypto — except that Warren looks likely to take the spot. Brown may well end up heading the Senate Agriculture Committee, which oversees the CFTC: the regulator crypto has spent the last two years trying to put in charge of its industry. 5
The House losses also cost crypto two of its most useful allies. Huizenga, the vice chair of the House Financial Services Committee, lost to Democrat Sean McCann. Steil, who chairs the committee’s digital assets subcommittee, lost to Democrat Mitchell Berman. Next year, House Financial Services will likely be run by Maxine Waters, 6 who is reportedly already working to expand her “Crypto Investor and Consumer Bill of Rights,” originally proposed as an amendment to the Clarity Act, into a full market structure bill of her own. The consumer protection focus alone suggests the industry won’t like it, but she’s also expected to fold in many of the Senate Democrats’ proposed Clarity amendments, which focused on enforcement and on ethics provisions aimed at the president. 7
Fairshake wasn’t the only crypto PAC that had a bad night. The Winklevoss-funded, explicitly pro-MAGA Digital Freedom Fund spent all of its $9 million across three Senate races, and lost all three. Most of it went to Ohio, where it added to Fairshake’s spending with its own $7.5 million to oppose Brown and support Husted. It also spent about $870,000 trying to stop James Talarico and boost Ken Paxton in Texas , and about $700,000 against Josh Turek and for Ashley Hinson in Iowa . First Principles Digital , a crypto PAC linked to Senator Cynthia Lummis, went 0 for 2: it spent $810,000 backing Mike Rogers against Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan , and $200,000 trying to unseat Democratic Representative Shomari Figures in Alabama’s 2nd District , a crypto ally the industry had helped elect in 2024. Across all crypto super PACs, they went one for six in competitive general election races. Of the $82.2 million that was spent on these races, 84% of it was spent on the losing side. In races rated safe or likely, they spent $9.1 million and went 29 for 29.
Now in fairness, this was a Democratic wave year, and some of these candidates might have lost regardless. But the crypto industry made little effort to prepare for that possibility. In 2024, even as many in the industry were confident Trump would win and Republicans would perform well in congressional elections, Fairshake still spent heavily on Democrats, including more than $10 million each to help elect Elissa Slotkin in Michigan and Ruben Gallego in Arizona. This cycle, despite polls predicting varying degrees of a Democratic wave for months, crypto went the other way. Outside of primaries, crypto super PACs did almost no significant spending to help Democrats. And the industry added new, explicitly partisan PACs this cycle. The Winklevoss-funded Digital Freedom Fund pledged to back “pro-Trump, pro-crypto champions” and to “stop Democrats from sabotaging the President’s agenda”; 8 the Cynthia Lummis-linked America First Digital network vowed to elect Republicans to give the industry a “trusted and effective voice”. 9 And despite Fairshake’s continual insistence that it’s bipartisan, its spending told a different story. It might have helped if the PAC had opposed even a single Republican in a Senate race, an industry source told CoinDesk . 10 Instead, crypto tied itself to the party that was about to lose, and is now hoping to convince everyone that it won.
The AI industry’s super PACs, which spent about $80 million this cycle, had problems too, though somewhat different ones. The Leading the Future network, funded by Andreessen Horowitz and OpenAI’s Greg Brockman, followed the same back-the-winners strategy as Fairshake. In the primaries, where it spent $27.8 million, only seven of 37 races were competitive. But its big win, in New York’s District 12 , was a costly one. It spent $8.2 million opposing Democrat Alex Bores, who ran on a platform of “AI safety”, only for Bores to lose to Micah Lasher, who campaigned on the same. All that money didn’t even buy the network an ally.
And the race mostly served to draw attention to how much AI super PACs were willing to spend to influence elections, further galvanizing voters already worried about AI and data centers in their communities. Backlash reached the PAC’s donors: Greg Brockman reportedly canceled a planned second $25 million donation amid pushback from OpenAI employees, a and venture capitalist Ron Conway, the network’s only publicly known Democratic funder, said he wouldn’t contribute again. 11
That backlash likely explains why the PAC largely sat out the general election: despite announced plans to dump $125 million into the midterms, 12 it ultimately spent less than a third of that. In the general election, it spent just under $10 million to back 21 sure winners. It supported only a single candidate in a competitive race: Darline Graham , who lost her South Carolina Senate race despite $4.8 million in support from the network — its largest general election expenditure and second largest overall.
A late entrant to the spending was the conservative, pro-AI Alliance for a Better Future , which advocates for “build[ing] superintelligence b for American families, shaped by American faith, and securing our American freedoms.” 13 Its advisory council lists members from groups including the Heritage Foundation , the NCOSE anti-pornography organization, and the effective altruist-aligned Future of Life Institute . c Its only participation came during the general election, where it spent $2.1 million on four Republican Senate candidates, all of whom lost: Mike Rogers in Michigan, Susan Collins in Maine , Ashley Hinson in Iowa, and Roger Marshall in Kansas .
The two factions of pro-AI super PAC spending
AI-focused PACs are far more fragmented in strategy than the crypto industry’s PACs. While the Leading the Future network and Alliance for a Better Future d represent the interests of AI companies that want to see little regulation, a handful of PACs emerged to oppose them and advocate for what they describe as “AI safety”. e The Public First network, funded mostly by a dark money group backed by Anthropic, spent $13.2 million of its $24.2 million in primary spending to back Alex Bores, who lost. Like Leading the Future, Public First largely stayed out of the general election, spending just $5 million across four races. Its clearest win came in California’s District 11 , where it spent $4.7 million to back Democrat Scott Wiener and oppose his Democratic opponents, Connie Chan and (in the primary) Saikat Chakrabarti. Wiener earned their support through his AI regulation credentials in the state senate, where he authored SB 1047 (a frontier AI regulation bill vetoed in 2024) and its narrower successor SB 53 (which became law).
Public First co-founder Brad Carson boasted of the network’s “crushing blow to the AI industry’s anti-regulatory efforts”, and the group drew some flattering coverage for its claim that 10 of the 17 congressional candidates it backed won their races. 11 But like Fairshake, Public First is overstating things by counting races rather than dollars.
