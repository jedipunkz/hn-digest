Guilty Until Proven Human: How AI Suspicion Broke Book Publishing
Jerry Falade kept the receipts. When his debut crime novel was pulled from under him in the last days of July 2026, the Nigerian chemistry doctoral candidate at Southern Methodist University in Dallas did not simply issue a denial. He itemised his evidence. The story, the characters, the scenes, the humour, the structure and the voice all came from him, he said, and he had time-stamped drafts, written notes and dated WhatsApp messages documenting the book's development.
Read that list again, because it is the most important document in the affair and it is not a document at all. It is a defence file, assembled by a first-time novelist from the digital exhaust of the act of writing. Draft timestamps. Notes. Messages to friends. It is the evidentiary apparatus of a fraud trial, produced by a man who had, a fortnight earlier, been the most sought-after debut author in American publishing.
There was no trial, no tribunal, no standard of proof, no appeal and no finding of fact. There was a two-book deal reported at 2.4 million dollars with Macmillan's Minotaur Books, won in a fourteen-way auction, and then on 29 July there was not. His agents at Europa Content withdrew the manuscript from submission. Marc Gerald, the agent who had championed it, said the agency had vetted the book with Falade for AI use during submission and could no longer substantiate what it had been told. “We feel terrible for putting this into the universe and the firestorm it has caused,” he said. Falade's account, the agency indicated, had changed in aspects.
Notice what is missing. Nobody said the book was written by a machine. The stated reason for the collapse was that the chain of custody could no longer be authenticated. The manuscript did not fail a test. It failed to be provable.
Fourteen Bidders and a Question Nobody Could Answer
The commercial logic underneath the collapse is less about literary ethics than most of the coverage suggested, and it explains why this will keep happening. Under United States copyright law, material generated by a machine without sufficient human authorship is not protectable, as the Copyright Office set out in its March 2023 registration guidance and has restated since. On 2 March 2026 the US Supreme Court declined to hear Thaler v. Perlmutter, leaving in place the D.C. Circuit's ruling that copyright requires a human author. A publisher paying seven figures for a novel is not buying a reading experience. It is buying an exclusive right, and an exclusive right with unknown holes in it may not be worth anything.
That is why the operative sentence in the whole episode was procedural rather than moral. Those handling the book said they were no longer able to authenticate how the manuscript had evolved from origin to completion. This is a due diligence failure, not a verdict. But it lands on the author with all the force of a verdict, because from the outside there is no way to distinguish a withdrawal for unprovability from a withdrawal for guilt, and the market does not bother trying.
Falade has consistently denied writing the book with AI. He acknowledged running an open-source model locally for research, including queries about the chemistry of body disposal, which is a reasonable thing for a chemistry doctoral student writing about a cartel fixer to look up without leaving a trail in a search engine. He has also said something the industry has been reluctant to engage with. “There seems to be a troubling assumption,” he said, “that when a Black writer produces work that attracts significant attention, the work could not possibly be their own.” And on the process: “I was on the verge of success, and then all of a sudden, there were rumors, but no one asked me anything.”
Here is the detail that should trouble anyone who thinks the answer is better software. According to Gerald, Europa did not run AI-detection tools on the manuscript, because at the time it found Falade's explanation clear and credible. Detectors were used, just not by anyone who owed him anything. Some industry publications said they ran the manuscript through Pangram, and Falade says a journalist submitted it to detection software without his knowledge or consent, producing a report that counted 18 “not X, but Y” constructions and 46 em dashes. The only instruments in the story were wielded by third parties with no duty to him, on an unpublished book, tallying stylistic tics. The people with obligations to him ran nothing. The industry did not fail because its instruments were bad. It failed because those responsible had no instruments, no procedure and no threshold, and in the absence of those things it fell back on the oldest mechanism available: reputational panic.
The Machines Can Tell and You Cannot
There is a body of research that speaks directly to the editor sitting with a manuscript and a bad feeling, and its findings are more unsettling than either the optimists or the pessimists in publishing have noticed.
In January 2026, a team from Chalmers University of Technology and Edinburgh Napier University published a study at the International Conference on Agents and Artificial Intelligence titled “Interpretable Text Classification Applied to the Detection of LLM-generated Creative Writing”. Minerva Suvanto, Andrea McGlinchey, Mattias Wahde and Peter J Barclay set out to answer a specific question: not merely whether machine-generated fiction can be detected, but why the detection works.
Their corpus, named DET12, was built from twelve public-domain detective novels, six by Agatha Christie and six by Dorothy L. Sayers, taken from Project Gutenberg. They chopped the texts into excerpts of roughly a hundred words, cutting on full stops, and produced 8,068 human-written samples. Each went to GPT-4.1 with an instruction to rewrite it while preserving the key details and approximate length, yielding 8,068 machine-written counterparts.
Then they tested people. Building on an earlier experiment in which nineteen evaluators managed 55 per cent accuracy, they recruited 119 evaluators and showed each five pairs of excerpts, one human and one machine, with ninety seconds and no internet access to decide which was which. That produced 595 judgements. Of those, 297 were correct. The accuracy rate was 49.9 per cent. Not poor. Not degraded. Indistinguishable from a coin.
The machine-learning classifiers, using nothing but single-word features drawn from a vocabulary of 30,302 tokens, achieved test accuracies between 0.9310 for a random forest and 0.9814 for the interpretable linear model the authors had designed for the purpose. Naive Bayes reached 0.9793 using an algorithm that predates the web.
That gap is the crisis of confidence in publishing rendered as a number. The person whose professional identity is built on reading closely performs at chance. A bag-of-words model from the 1960s performs at ninety-eight per cent. Every editor who has ever said “I can just tell” is, on this evidence, telling you about their intuition rather than about the text.
What the Classifier Was Actually Reading
The reason the paper matters is that its authors did not stop at the accuracy figure. They built an interpretable model precisely so they could open it up, and what they found inside is far more awkward for publishing than the headline result.
The dominant signal was lexical variety. Where Christie or Sayers wrote “said”, the rewrite reached for “remarked”, “responded” or “mentioned”; “cried” became “exclaimed”; plain adjectives such as good, bad, great and beautiful gave way to more elaborate alternatives. The authors measured this information-theoretically and found the machine text carried 9.164 bits of unigram entropy against 8.875 for the human originals, implying roughly 22 per cent greater variation in word choice. The machine is not writing worse. It is writing with a bigger thesaurus and less restraint, and that alone gives it away.
But four further categories emerged from the hand annotation, and three of them should stop any editor cold.
The second was temporal drift. The human corpus was written in the 1920s and 1930s, and it uses the spellings of that period, including “to-day” and “to-morrow”. The machine modernises them. The authors offer one word as an illustration. The verb “exited” barely appears in English before about 1960. It occurs zero times across the human samples and 61 times in the machine rewrites of the training set.
The third category, which the paper labels English varieties, is the one that ought to be printed and pinned above every commissioning desk in London. The authors note that the human texts were written by British authors, and that the model “exhibits a preference for Americanisms”. The example given in their table is the pair realise and realize.
The fourth and fifth were foreign language and colloquialism. The Christie and Sayers originals carry passages of French and contractions such as “'em” for “them”, and the model strips both. The authors observe, in a sentence that reads like a lament, that such features “often make a novel more vivid and interesting to read”.
So the ninety-eight per cent accuracy is real, and it is partly an artefact of the fact that one class was written by British novelists a century ago and the other by an American model last year. The classifier has learned, among other things, that British spelling and dated idiom mean human. The authors are candid that their rewriting methodology may itself have inflated the synonym variety, and that prompting a model to compose from an outline would be a different test. They report preliminary work suggesting detectability survives, but say plainly that further work is needed.
Now transplant that classifier into a slush pile.
Arithmetic That Publishing Has Not Done
The DET12 experiment is a balanced binary forced choice. Half the samples are machine-written by construction, and the classifier is asked to sort a closed set in which the prior probability is exactly fifty per cent.
Manuscript screening is not that problem. It is a rare-event screening problem, and the difference is the difference between a useful tool and a machine for generating accusations.
Take a mid-sized imprint receiving ten thousand submissions a year, and assume, generously to the detector, that five per cent contain substantial machine-written prose. Run a detector with the DET12 linear model's characteristics: it correctly flags 490 of the 500, and also flags two per cent of the 9,500 clean ones, which is 190 authors. Of the 680 manuscripts in the flagged pile, 190 belong to writers who did nothing. Roughly one in four accusations is false.
Drop the prevalence to one per cent, plausible for agented submissions from established writers, and the arithmetic becomes grotesque. Ninety-eight true positives sit alongside 198 false ones. Most of the accused are innocent. The tool is still ninety-eight per cent accurate, and worse than useless, because the two errors are not symmetrical: missing a machine-written book costs a publisher an unenforceable copyright, while falsely flagging a human one ends a career.
This is not a hypothetical failure mode. It is a documented one, and it has already played out at scale in another industry entirely.
The Academy Already Ran This Experiment and Lost
Turnitin launched its AI writing detector in April 2023 and, within four months, had run it across more than 38 million student submissions. The company initially claimed a false positive rate of around one per cent at document level. It later acknowledged that at sentence level the figure was closer to four per cent.
Vanderbilt University did the arithmetic that publishing has not. Noting that it had submitted around 75,000 papers to Turnitin in 2022, the university's Center for Teaching observed that a one per cent false positive rate would have meant roughly 750 students wrongly implicated
