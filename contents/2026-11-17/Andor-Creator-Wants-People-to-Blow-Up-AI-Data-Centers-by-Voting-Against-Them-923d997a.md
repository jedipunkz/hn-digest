"There's probably an emotional amplitude knob on some f***ing algorithm."
Star Wars writer Tony Gilroy has sounded the alarm over the rising tide of AI slop that he believes is a threat to human-made movies.
Speaking to Vanity Fair , Gilroy implored movie fans to vote against the spread of AI data centers — and even joking suggested that the artificial intelligence-fuelling tech hubs should be blown up.
Gilroy, who co-wrote Star Wars: Rogue One and created its widely-praised spinoff Andor, was speaking at a panel event to promote his new film Behemoth! , which stars Pedro Pascal as a cellist who records Hollywood film scores and begins having an existential crisis.
"People's natural resistance to the robot takeover is clear," Gilroy said regarding the recent surge in AI's capabilities — and the concerns surrounding the technology's cultural, financial, and ecological impact. "I don't know what's going to happen. I really don't. The music scene out here [in Los Angeles] is the coral reef of what's happened. It's been dying out here for the last 15 years... It's grooving a little bit now. I think people want humans."
Ultimately, Gilmore suggested, the only way that the rampant spread of AI creation will be curbed is via a public pushback to the building of more AI data centers, which are currently needed to fuel the rise in computational power that generative AI must gobble.
"Blow up the AI centers, man," Gilmore said, prompting Pascal to joke that Gilmore wasn't actually suggesting citizens should attempt domestic terrorism. "I don't mean to [actually] blow them up," Gilmore clarified. "I mean blow them up at the voting box. Go and vote and blow them up. Go see the Musk documentary, man. Go see that, watch the whole four hours. I'm pitching my movie [here], but watch that movie."
Here, Gilmore is refering to Musk, the Alex Gibney-directed four-hour unvarnished analysis of the SpaceX and Tesla leader, who also has a stake in AI via X/Twitter and its tool Grok.
"I don't know what's going to come back, but this is this incredibly humanizing element of movies that's happened," Gilmore concluded. "Can it be replaced by AI? Probably, quite shockingly, simply. There's probably an emotional amplitude knob on some f***ing algorithm, but this movie is organic."
Last month, OpenAI boss Sam Altman sparked a backlash when he said he thought it was “terrible” for people to have RAM in their PCs rather than a data center. Speaking to YouTuber Cheeky Pint, Altman said: "From a global allocation perspective, actually, it's quite terrible to have everybody have a bunch of the world's limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer, and being used 5% of the time. Like, you want maximum utilization in the cloud if you're trying to deal with this very supply constrained environment. And somehow many people's intuition is the opposite of that."
The comments were widely ridiculed, with many suggesting that, whether he intended it or not, Altman sounded like he wanted to take away personal ownership of memory and force society to use resources for the benefit of AI companies such as his. Altman later insisted his comments had been taken out of context .
Data centers and the potential impact on jobs and humanity’s very existence from AI are key issues for voters, and are thought to have proven crucial during the recent midterm elections in the U.S. Meanwhile, the AI boom has led to a scarcity of memory to power it, which in turn has caused graphics cards, video game consoles, and PC components to skyrocket in price. Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo have all raised the prices of their consoles as a result of the ‘RAMpocalypse,’ while the RTX 5080 just disappeared off of Nvidia's marketplace, weeks after the RTX 5090 was all but discontinued.
