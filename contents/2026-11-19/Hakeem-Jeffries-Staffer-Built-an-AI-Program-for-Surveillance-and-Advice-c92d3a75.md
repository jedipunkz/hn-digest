‘What Should the Leader Say?’: Hakeem Jeffries Staffer Built an AI Program for Surveillance and Advice
The minority leader’s office experimented with an AI program coded to advise Jeffries on AI policy, news events and opponents.
(Photo illustration by Mark Alfred/The Washington Sun; AP)
A member of House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries’ staff operated a custom-designed artificial intelligence-powered program dubbed “Caucus Pulse” to surveil various factions of the Democratic caucus and suggest messaging for Jeffries, The Washington Sun has learned.
The staffer for Jeffries — who easily won a vote Wednesday teeing him up to become the next speaker of the House — also paid Elon Musk’s X to download tens of thousands of tweets posted by the official and personal accounts of House lawmakers using lists made by an anonymous X account linked to Jeffries’ team: BigBaseballFan56.
The Caucus Pulse program, which was largely produced and fueled by Anthropic’s AI agent Claude, also generated a detailed report recommending steps Jeffries could take as minority leader to address concerns about the rapid development of Anthropic’s AI technology.
In a statement to The Sun, Jeffries’ office said the minority leader himself wasn’t aware of Caucus Pulse and didn’t use material generated by the AI-powered program.

“Just like people from all walks of life do everyday, a staffer in this office conducted a short-lived experiment with AI in a personal capacity, without the involvement of the Office of the Democratic Leader,” Jeffries spokesperson Christie Stephenson said. “No official resources were used, and we’re addressing this matter internally.”
“It is clear that tools powered by artificial intelligence have utility,” Stephenson continued. “That’s why the House of Representatives approves the official use of AI to build out office capacity and advance the goals of Member offices in a fast-paced environment. Several staff members in this office participate in the use of AI tools in full accordance with House guidelines and privacy policies.”
The Sun discovered Caucus Pulse’s source code in a public GitHub repository alongside files containing AI-generated instructions for the AI program to follow. The program is unambiguous in its purpose : “You are a research analyst in the House Democratic Leader’s research and writing shop, writing for staff who have thirty seconds.”
The Caucus Pulse program was used to track a variety of topics — including instances of racism against Jeffries and which lawmakers came to his aid — according to roughly six weeks of publicly viewable data starting on Sept. 1 and running through mid-October.
In the GitHub repository’s documents were questions put to AI about newsworthy events, including: “what should the Leader say?”
After AI researcher Jacob Coxon publicly resigned from Anthropic because of concerns that artificial intelligence programs “could kill us all” by the decade’s end, the program generated a memo recommending Jeffries pursue the “hearings/oversight” on AI floated by members of his party “rather than a bill.”
In the Sept. 11 memo, Caucus Pulse suggested Jeffries avoid echoing the former Anthropic researcher’s concerns given the company’s donations to Democrats. Instead, the AI program suggested he push for hearings and oversight — a conclusion it reached by analyzing the tweets of other Democratic lawmakers.
Stephenson said that neither Jeffries nor his senior staff were aware of the AI tool, which she said was only tested for a short period of time “before it was neglected and ultimately abandoned after a few weeks.”
Stephenson added that House policy allows staffers to use AI in order to “monitor and collate public information, including what the American people are seeing and reading.”
The GitHub repository, designed using Anthropic’s Claude and OpenAI’s ChatGPT, was made private shortly after The Sun sent a request for comment to Jeffries’ office.
The repository details steps taken by its creator to keep both the codebase and its anonymous X account from being visibly associated with his office. The GitHub account was set up via a “Hide My Email” relay address, a feature that allows Apple users to generate an anonymous email account.
Buried within updates to the program code is the personal email of Jacob Stoken, the legislative and policy aide in Jeffries’ office responsible for Caucus Pulse. His name is also on some updates to the code, with the AI assistant Claude listed as a co-author. In a program description field, Caucus Pulse describes one of its core principles as: “intelligence everywhere.”
A view of the Caucus Pulse program built by a Jeffries staffer to monitor factions of the Democratic caucus and their social media engagement. (Source: GitHub)
Come January, Jeffries is set to stand second in line to the presidency. He’ll also become one of the most powerful lawmakers in a Congress primed to address the breakneck pace of AI development and its consequences for the nation.
Jeffries has offered few specifics on what type of AI regulation he supports.
But he’s demonstrated interest in AI matters of late, announcing last year the creation of a House Democratic Commission on AI and the Innovation Economy. He’s also praised AI giant Anthropic, a major government contractor that’s found itself crosswise with President Donald Trump after it pushed back against the possibility the government would use its products for mass surveillance.
While Stephenson said that official resources were not used on Caucus Pulse, Jeffries’ office has spent around $5,300 on tools from OpenAI and Anthropic since the beginning of the 119th Congress, according to data provided by his office.
On Sept. 10, Caucus Pulse reported spending $126.84 on X posts, with a ceiling budget of “$250/day.” The use of Claude is, at a minimum, “$90/month” with a ceiling budget of $40 a day.
Since April 2023 — long before Caucus Pulse became active — Jeffries’ team appears to have maintained the anonymous X account @Baseball_Fan_56.
At least 13 current or former staffers and interns in Jeffries’ district and minority leader’s office follow some of the lists created by the @Baseball_Fan_56 X account, which bears a picture of retired New York Yankee Derek Jeter.
BigBaseballFan56 now follows 14 accounts, having unfollowed a Washington Sun reporter after The Sun inquired about its existence. (Source: X)
BigBaseballFan56 has been used to track officials and media since the Biden administration, and follows news sites such as ABC, CNN and FOX as well as Derek Jeter himself. (Jeffries once joked that the House should subpoena the Baseball Hall of Fame to determine who voted against the now-Hall of Famer’s induction.)
Caucus Pulse paid X per post for the use of its application programming interface, or API, to download tweets from hundreds of social media accounts, mostly the professional and personal accounts of House lawmakers and lists of journalists maintained in the program’s code.
In the weeks of viewable data The Sun analyzed, the program paid X to read 114,647 posts and viewed 69,285 user accounts. There are nearly 2 million lines of code across the entire system, which is described as a “permanent, growing corpus” of posts with “phrase tracking, and a daily report.”
The program identifies “developing stories” based on the downloaded X posts. It then asks Claude to answer the following questions:
Is this breaking through beyond our own echo?
Who is carrying it — members, caucuses, leadership, outside accounts on no List?
What is the GOP saying back, how fast, or are they silent?
Is the claim true and what is the sourcing?
Where did it start and who started it?
What is the hole, and what should the Leader say?
At another point, the program is asked to find holes in Republicans’ statements and prepare a retort for Jeffries: “mark which GOP posts answer which caucus line even when they avoid our words, find the hole, and draft one line the Leader could use.”
The Caucus Pulse program appears to have a function that identified racism aimed at the minority leader, with one line of code reading:
label: "Racist attack on Leader Jeffries ('monkey', 'errand boy')."
The program has also drawn from a nongovernment email account — “tobriefornottobrief” — that compiles news information from some free newsletters and a paid newsletter, Politico Pro.
In generating its recommendations report on the Anthropic researcher’s resignation, the program paid X per post downloaded and Anthropic was paid for the use of Claude Code. It was not clear where the funds originated; Stephenson said no tax dollars were used.
“The memo was not requested by or reviewed by any staff member,” Stephenson said. “The Leader was briefed on the AI-related news development in question by his staff.”
Caucus Pulse’s advice to Jeffries: pursue the “hearings/oversight” on AI floated by members of his party “rather than a bill.” The AI model recommended that Jeffries not advance legislation to address Anthropic’s rapid AI advancements.
The memo said that the minority leader should not rely on the Anthropic researcher Coxon’s warning in his messaging, noting that the Democratic response was already being called a “donor operation” online.
“Anthropic’s sponsorships/PAC ties make any Democrat carrying the company’s safety line vulnerable to the ‘regulatory capture’ frame,” Claude wrote.
