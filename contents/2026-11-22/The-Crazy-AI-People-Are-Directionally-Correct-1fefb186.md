The nature and limits of responsible AI scepticism
Dan Williams Nov 22, 2026 71 4 24 Share Many people working on AI and AI safety, or involved with the rationalist and effective altruist communities, have coalesced around the following views:
Imminent recursive self-improvement (RSI) . Within a few years, AI systems will likely automate most of the work involved in building better AI systems . The resulting feedback loop will then dramatically speed up AI progress .
Imminent superintelligence . This process will quickly produce systems far more capable than even the most capable humans at virtually every cognitive task.
Imminent transformation . Where previous general-purpose technologies like electricity or the internet took decades to change how we live, superintelligence will radically transform the world very shortly after arriving: perhaps a century of technological progress compressed into a decade , or rates of economic growth without historical precedent .
Call this the “ superintelligence perspective ”.
Each claim combines a view about the direction in which AI is heading with a view about speed and scale. 1
If the superintelligence perspective is true, it is perhaps the most important truth in the history of humanity.
It would be nice to dismiss it as the ramblings of Elizabeth Holmes-style charlatans, but that would be wrong.
For one thing, the perspective is taken seriously by many very smart, very well-informed people, including AI pioneers and people with impressive forecasting records . It is not just people working in the companies who endorse the superintelligence perspective, although tech leaders and AI engineers who endorse it should not be completely dismissed either. (Unlike Holmes, they have built perhaps the fastest-growing industry in human history on the basis of a technology that verifiably works across countless domains.)
More importantly, the perspective is supported by persuasive arguments and plausible extrapolations from existing trends. The length of certain software tasks that frontier AI systems can complete with 50% reliability, measured by how long the tasks take skilled humans, has been doubling roughly every seven months since 2019, and closer to every three to four months since 2024 . This year alone, AI systems have found thousands of previously unknown vulnerabilities in widely used software and produced a computer-checked proof that appears to have solved one of mathematics’ Millennium Prize problems. According to an assessment by the research organisation METR published recently, Anthropic’s models have been improving on benchmarks at nearly twice their previous rate since October 2025, with most of the current acceleration probably due to AI itself.
Just this week, a mathematician at Anthropic announced that, working with Claude, he had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, another Millennium Prize problem and perhaps the most famous open problem in all of mathematics. (This has now been independently verified .) This might be the most important advance in mathematics in a century.
On capabilities, the forecasting record also seems to favour the crazy AI people (I use the term affectionately) over many of their critics. In a 2022 forecasting tournament , for example, a group of superforecasters—those with a proven track record of successful prediction—assigned only a 2.3% chance to an AI system achieving a gold-medal score at the International Mathematical Olympiad by 2025. It happened in July 2025. By contrast, a scenario that Daniel Kokotajlo, the lead author of “AI 2027”, wrote in 2021 about how AI would develop up to 2026 has held up surprisingly well .
So, could the superintelligence perspective actually be true?

In debates about AI, I gravitate towards what I will call “ responsible AI scepticism ”.
In saying this is responsible , I mean it faces up to the reality of rapid AI capabilities progress. It doesn’t dismiss today’s systems as stochastic parrots or fancy auto-complete, claim that AI can’t do things it can do or is clearly on track to be able to do, or confidently predict that deep learning is about to “ hit a wall ”.
Most fundamentally, it doesn’t assume that human capabilities have mysterious underlying mechanisms that couldn’t, in principle, be replicated in machines.
It is sceptical of the superintelligence perspective, however.
There are many such responsible sceptics , including people I have recently interviewed on my podcast, such as Steven Pinker , Séb Krier , and Arvind Narayanan .
One aim I have here is to review some of the reasons that go into this responsible scepticism, which I am broadly sympathetic to. But another aim, which is in some ways more important, is to highlight the limits of this sceptical perspective.
For one thing, none of the reasons for scepticism that I am aware of constitute knockdown arguments. This means that responsible sceptics must allow that the crazy AI people might be right. And this should weigh heavily on collective decision making.
For another, these arguments show, at most, that the superintelligence perspective is wrong about speed and scale: that recursive self-improvement will be slower, superintelligence more uneven and less powerful, and AI-driven transformations more gradual than its proponents expect. But the strongest such arguments don’t dispute that its proponents are directionally correct. And this fact has huge importance.
The Case for Responsible Scepticism
Two kinds of reasons for scepticism are general.
The first concerns our “priors” (i.e., what we should expect before looking at the specific evidence).
Crazy things rarely happen. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. These are basic principles of rationality.
The superintelligence perspective predicts the largest and fastest transformation in human history occurring in the next few years. Changes on anything like the scale it imagines have happened only twice, with farming and then the industrial revolution, and neither happened anywhere near as quickly. Forecasts of imminent upheaval, by contrast, are very common and usually wrong, even when they come from communities of high-IQ people with rational arguments they deem compelling.
So, before looking at any of the details, we should think the perspective is probably wrong.
Forecasters sometimes call this taking the “outside view” , which asks what happened in relevantly similar cases, as opposed to the “inside view”, which builds predictions from the specifics of a case.
How likely are you to get divorced? An inside view would look at the specifics of your marriage. An outside view would look at what percentage of married couples in a similar situation get divorced.
Of course, outside-view reasoning can only get you so far. And here, proponents of the superintelligence perspective will point out that they are not engaging in any galaxy-brained reasoning for a crazy conclusion; they are simply extrapolating robust trends, especially about capabilities growth.
However, the question concerns which trends we should extrapolate. Most of the trends on which the superintelligence perspective rests are relatively recent (a decade or so old). This includes not just rapid, plausibly exponential capabilities progress but exponential increases in the revenue of frontier AI companies. (Anthropic is probably the fastest-growing company in the history of capitalism .)
In contrast, longer-running stable trends should plausibly be given more weight, in part because they have survived more tests. For example, US GDP per person has grown at roughly 2% a year for around 150 years , despite many profound technological advances during this period (e.g., electrification, cars, computers, and the internet).
Moreover, not even all of AI’s own trends point towards an imminent explosion or transformative revolution. Recent progress has required inputs—chips, money, human researchers—growing at rates that plausibly can’t continue for much longer . AI’s footprint in the economy is also still small, with the share of American work hours that involve generative AI at only about 5% .
As many have pointed out , markets seem to share this scepticism. If investors expected explosive growth within a decade, they would plausibly save less and borrow more, pushing long-term real interest rates far above anything on record , which isn’t what we see.
More broadly, the whole economy looks surprisingly normal-ish if we are on the cusp of imminent world-transforming superintelligence.
The second reason concerns the collective “epistemics” of the community that developed the superintelligence perspective: that is, its reliability in producing accurate judgements and forecasts. Here, one doesn’t need to endorse hyperbolic discourse accusing the community of being a cult to be suspicious about its highly insular nature.
The perspective has been created and refined within a surprisingly small, weirdly densely connected social world with its own institutions, funders, vocabulary, and peculiar ingroup signals.
To give you just a taste of how strangely small this world is: when Open Philanthropy (now Coefficient Giving), the biggest philanthropic funder in the AI safety world, pledged OpenAI $30 million in 2017, it disclosed that two OpenAI researchers who advised it, Dario Amodei and Paul Christiano, lived in the same house as its executive director, Holden Karnofsky, who was engaged to Amodei’s sister. The Amodei siblings went on to co-found Anthropic, Karnofsky later joined it , and Christiano now sits on the board of OpenAI’s foundation.
Christiano is married to Ajeya Cotra, who worked for nearly a decade at Open Philanthropy and is one of the most influential voices in the rationalist, effective altruist, and AI safety worlds. She is now a researcher at METR , an organisation whose findings (e.g., about exponentially increasing capabilities and recursive self-improvement ) play perhaps the biggest role in the entire information environment in lending support to the superintelligence perspective. METR began as a team inside a research centre founded by… Christiano.
I could go on for much longer, but it would be tedious and these things can be very easily googled (or Claude-d).
Obviously none of this proves that the community is wrong. Insular communities bound together by shared views, values, and ingroup markers can develop correct beliefs and conduct excellent research, and this one has an impressive track record. Nevertheless, when you think about a topic, you should consider not just the strength of object-level findings and arguments but whether the distribution of those findings and arguments is likely to be skewed towards particular conclusions.
In the case of the superintelligence perspective, there are legitimate reasons for concern: a community with strong shared priors that plausibly constitute a signal of group membership will produce lots of clever arguments and intellectual support for those beliefs whether or not they are true. And this should increase our scepticism of these arguments even absent specific objections to them. It should also influence how we interpret the findings of research organisations like METR that are claimed to be “independent” of frontier AI companies such as Anthropic and OpenAI whose technology they evaluate, not because of any ill-intent or incompetence on the part of the excellent researchers there but simply because their formal independence co-exists with highly overlapping worldviews and many informal social connections.
