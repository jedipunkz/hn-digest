We prove that every arithmetic circuit that computes the permanent of an 𝑚 × 𝑚 matrix over the reals has more than 𝑚^{𝑐(log 𝑚)^{1/3}} gates, for some constant 𝑐 > 0 and all sufficiently large 𝑚. 1 anthropics/formal-math Cite Context readers might want to know
VP vs VNP is the algebraic analogue of P vs NP . The two are separate problems. There is no known way to turn the separation claimed here into a proof that P ≠ NP, so the result gives no clear indication of how close we are to settling P vs NP.
The claim would settle a question Leslie Valiant posed in 1979: can we prove that some explicit polynomials are inherently expensive to compute? For decades, researchers could rule out efficient calculations under restrictions such as a fixed number of layers. This paper claims to rule out every polynomial-size arithmetic circuit for one such problem, however many layers it uses or intermediate results it reuses.

Copy Edit The permanent of a square matrix looks almost identical to the determinant. Both are sums over all ways of pairing the rows of an m × m m \times m m × m matrix with its columns, where each pairing contributes the product of the chosen entries. The determinant attaches alternating signs to those products, and that small change makes it computable by arithmetic circuits of polynomial size. The permanent has no such sign pattern, and whether circuits built from additions and multiplications can compute it efficiently is a central open question.
This paper reports a superpolynomial lower bound for such circuits. The authors do not attack the permanent head-on. They study auxiliary polynomials that encode networks of local constraints, prove that computing those polynomials requires large circuits, and then use the fact that a permanent circuit can be converted into a circuit for those polynomials. The result is about formal arithmetic computation, where a circuit's gates use addition and multiplication and must compute the right polynomial over the field and its extensions. [p 1 ] That is different from Boolean computation, where a circuit only needs to be right on 0/1 inputs. [p 1 ] The paper does not solve P vs NP. [p 1 ]

From circuits to a geometric fork
The proof builds a bridge between circuit size and geometry. A tensor is an array with an index for each "party." A "wire" links two parties, and a spectral point is a compatible way to assign values to such tensors. Such a point induces a pseudometric, a distance-like function, on the parties. A circuit's rank bound across a split of the parties can be represented by routing through two "hubs," and the wire lengths and hub separation then constrain the circuit's size. [p 6 ]
The geometry yields two routes. In the flat case, Euclidean-like configurations are available. In the contracting case, a fixed contraction inequality is available. [p 5 ] The authors prove that at least one of the two hypotheses holds over every field, but they do not know which, even over R \mathbb R R , or whether they are mutually exclusive. [p 6 ] [p 6 ]
Dense networks force the flat-case bound
The auxiliary polynomial in the flat case is built on a complete graph with k k k nodes, with an ℓ \ell ℓ -bit string on every edge. Each term chooses, at every node, a variable indexed by its incident edge strings, and the edge string forces the two endpoint choices to agree. Its coefficient tensor is therefore a product of wires. In a path network, as in iterated matrix multiplication, a balanced split can be crossed by a single wire. The circuit enters through a decomposition. The auxiliary polynomial is linear in each node's variable block, and a circuit with L L L gates writes it as pieces indexed by balanced sets, each with coefficient-tensor rank at most L L L across its cut. Hub routing turns that rank into a length cost. Under the flat hypothesis, one can place edge ends and hubs in a Euclidean-like configuration so that the network's total wire length exceeds the routed costs, which forces L L L to be large. The contracting case: a speed-up that cannot exist
In the contracting case the authors first prove a cutting theorem for nondeterministic programs whose accepting runs carry weights: a computation can be decomposed into shorter pieces that a faster program can list. A small permanent circuit would, via the clause-sum reduction and a low-degree evaluation argument, yield an "accelerator" that computes sums over short weighted runs in fewer steps. The authors propagate this putative speed-up to larger scales and a diagonal argument refutes it. [p 9 ] This is a contradiction in the proof, not an observed runtime improvement.
The determinant is the natural skeptical comparison. The authors say the proof breaks at the reduction from clause sums to the permanent: they know no comparable reduction for the determinant, which indeed has polynomial-size circuits. [p 10 ] The Boolean analogue fails differently: a circuit that is correct only on Boolean inputs can compute a different polynomial elsewhere, so the formal-polynomial properties the proof inspects need not hold. [p 10 ] This is why the argument separates algebraic classes without separating Boolean ones. The paper says the result was discovered by an internal research Claude model and formalized in Lean. [p 1 ] It also says no human mathematician has checked the proofs. [p 11 ] Every numbered statement except the determinantal-complexity consequence in Remark N.6 has a Lean counterpart. [p 11 ] Lean checks a formal proof against its formal statement; it does not establish that the statement says what the paper intends.
