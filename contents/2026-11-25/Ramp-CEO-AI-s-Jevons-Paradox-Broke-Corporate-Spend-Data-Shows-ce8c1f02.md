Ramp co-CEO Eric Glyman told Y Combinator president Garry Tan on the November 18 episode of How to Build the Future that "over the last two months, Jevons paradox broke" for AI, citing Ramp's direct visibility into what tens of thousands of U.S. businesses actually spend on OpenAI and Anthropic. Tan posted the clip to X on November 24, landing it in a market that has spent the month repricing AI infrastructure exposure.
Garry Tan (@garrytan) on X, November 24, 2026:
"Ramp credit card spending data suggests recently Jevon's Paradox broke for AI. AI inference per unit of intelligence keeps coming down but usage is not spiking up accordingly of late."
Glyman's headline number: OpenAI and Anthropic revenue from Ramp customers grew roughly 1-2% last month. The month before, it grew roughly 40%. Glyman also made the same observation one day earlier at the CNBC Digital Finance Forum on November 17.
The Demand Ceiling Glyman Describes
Jevons paradox holds that efficiency gains in resource consumption tend to increase total consumption rather than reduce it, because lower cost unlocks new uses. For AI, that meant every token price cut was supposed to pay for itself in volume: cheaper intelligence, more tasks automated, more spend in aggregate. That logic has been the bulls' answer to every price cut for two years.
Glyman says models have now crossed the capability threshold most business work actually requires, and the math inverted.
"If you have a job that requires 120 IQ, and every time that you go from 70 to 90 to 115, the returns to that are going to be incredible. But there's this question of what happens when you go from like 120 to 150 for tasks that don't require it."
Tan's framing was blunter: "RSI is here. The model capabilities are going like this. And then suddenly, like the capability of society to absorb that is more or less hit a limit."
Glyman was careful not to call this permanent. "It may not be permanent. Maybe we're at like a local maxima for now," he said, adding that new model families could reset the ceiling. But the data, in his read, is unambiguous right now.
The concentration data sharpens the concern. Ramp's figures show the top 1% of its business customers account for roughly 80% of all AI token spend, a cohort Glyman confirmed is mostly venture-backed tech firms. Inside Ramp itself, the same split held: 1% of employees were consuming 80% of tokens.
That is a thin, fragile demand base already under internal ROI scrutiny, not broad-based enterprise adoption.
Ramp's response internally has been to build toward cheaper defaults. Its AI system, Glass, routes tasks to simpler models where frontier capability is unnecessary. "We are happy to have the process and reasoning take 10% longer for a 50% cut in price," Glyman said. When a $60 billion company with $2 billion in annual revenue is actively optimizing down the cost stack, the lab revenue curve bends hard.
What This Means for the Infrastructure Buildout
The AI capex cycle has a specific chain of logic: cheaper tokens, more usage, more lab revenue, justified datacenter construction, sustained power demand growth. Glyman's data breaks the second link.
That matters far beyond lab earnings. The AI capex surge has been the single largest demand-side argument for new U.S. power generation for two years. Bitcoin miners compete directly with datacenters for power purchase agreements, grid interconnection, and cheap stranded energy. Forecasts of record power demand through 2027 rest on the assumption of exponential token consumption growth.
If corporate demand has lost its price-elasticity multiplier, those projections are running on a broken input.
A datacenter overbuild does not show up overnight. The stranded capacity becomes visible over 12 to 18 months as construction completes into a demand curve that isn't accelerating. At that point, the energy competition story that has been driving up costs and crowding miners off low-cost sites looks materially overstated. Cheap power may come back to the market faster than the current buildout narrative suggests.
The macro credit angle is harder to dismiss. Trillions in AI infrastructure bonds and equity have been underwritten on exponential demand growth. If demand is flat-to-linear on a per-dollar-of-capex basis, return assumptions collapse, credit tightens, and the repricing hits risk assets broadly.
Token volume on Ramp is still up roughly 27% week-over-week per Ramp AI Index data, so what broke is the multiplier, not spending outright. A lot of paper was written assuming that multiplier held forever.
The falsifiable test is Q1 2027 data from Ramp or comparable corporate spend trackers. If token volume accelerates again at a rate meaningfully exceeding price cuts, the paradox is restored and the capex thesis survives.
Glyman himself said a next-generation model family could reset the capability ceiling. Watch whether the labs' next major releases produce a measurable re-acceleration in Ramp's month-over-month spend figures. If they do not, the structural read hardens.
