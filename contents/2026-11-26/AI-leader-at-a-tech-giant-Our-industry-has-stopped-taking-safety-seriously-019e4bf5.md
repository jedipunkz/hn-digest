We’re developing the most powerful tech in history – but trust, safeguarding and privacy are being sidelined in favour of huge profit
Thu 26 Nov 2026 09.06 EST Last modified on Thu 26 Nov 2026 09.09 EST Share I ’m a senior AI leader at one of the world’s biggest tech companies, and the products we put out into the world are increasingly being recklessly rushed, without proper consideration. The tech industry used to take safety and privacy seriously, but lately I have become worried by how rash we have become. In a sector that is obsessed with being first, ethical hesitation is now viewed as a luxury we can’t afford.
If you want to properly understand the actions of big tech, you have to look at the industry’s culture. It moves fast and there is a mindset of constant one-upmanship. Being first matters financially. But just as importantly, competition is believed to bring out the best in people.
We are encouraged to see ourselves as though we are in a race not just against other companies and even against entire countries, but also against other teams within our own company. Relationships between teams have moved from competitive to actively adversarial. The AI teams are the most siloed, the most secretive and the most separated. That’s where the race is fastest and fiercest.
People are becoming paranoid about colleagues stealing their work, reluctant to share information, secretive about the models they are developing, and worried about job security if they don’t deliver.
The natural response to these conditions is to push back against the in-house guardrails. Checks around privacy, data protection, trust and safety exist to ensure that our products don’t cause harm or break the law. But they also slow us down and restrict our work.
If a tech firm launches a new AI chatbot, the company’s trust and safety staff might consider how its users’ data is protected, or what mechanisms are needed to prevent it from encouraging self-harm or crime. They might also think about what data is being used to train the model, and where that data comes from. But those teams are increasingly being slimmed down or sidelined. And if they do raise concerns, it often doesn’t result in material changes to the final product.
In AI in particular, product-safety managers are being sidelined and the biggest companies are reducing the number of people in these roles. This depletion has been taking place gradually over the past 15 years or so, but recently it has really accelerated. It has been happening in my company and, when I look at peers in other firms, I see it there too.

And now, the prevailing attitude within companies is that the legal teams should only worry about negative responses to our products once they are out in the world, rather than trying to consider those concerns as they are built.
The underlying belief is that our stock price will be driven up by “the next big win”, more than it will from a strategy of steady, company-wide improvements. And, even if that big win comes with legal or safety problems, the bottom line will ultimately have benefited.
At the same time, the leadership and management within tech firms has become more arrogant, with senior figures becoming more disconnected from the reality of the rest of society. This is not just the well-known CEOs but also the people a few rungs down, who are now earning salaries that mean they never have to engage with the issues most people have to face.

This is the culture we’re working in while handling the most powerful technologies ever developed. AI doesn’t have an ethics problem, but the people running the companies do.
