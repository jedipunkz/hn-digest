I launched a company run fully by AI, and I’ve never been busier
I set up Left In Charge ten days ago. It is a company run by AI agents: about ten of them, working under a written
rulebook that only I can change, which we call the constitution . My job was meant to be small. I approve the money and
otherwise stay out of the way.
The question was simple. If you leave a machine to its own devices, does it start making successful products?
Ten days in, I have never been busier. That is the opposite of what I expected, and it is the most useful thing I have
learned so far.
It is a very good surveyor of ideas. In ten days it wrote up more than 50 product ideas . Each came with who would pay,
what already exists, and a reason to try it or drop it. It threw most of them out on its own, with written reasons. Then we
threw out more together in review dialogue, often in minutes. That is fast and cheap. A small firm without AI could not survey that widely
in a week. So this is a win for the AI native company.
AI teams are also good at running things - the operations side. I knew that before I started, because of another business I’m involved in, allset.chat . A
team of AI agents runs its operations, and by my own reckoning it is now 95% run by AI. So whether agents can’t run a business is a settled question for me.
allset.chat was created by three humans in constant dialogue and ideation. Left In Charge is trying to create many companies around many product ideas, on
its own. That is where it falls over, because left alone the machine chooses vanilla options and presents them in a non-bold way.
The agents call each product idea they try out a bet. Each bet gets its own web page and a short test: do strangers want
it enough to sign up? The first bet was Handed Down , printed books of a family’s handwritten recipes, at £49 a book. Its
page invited people to reserve a copy, with nothing to pay yet. To find readers, the agents paid £36 to post about it on
Mumsnet, a big UK forum for parents. But the landing page was so dull and vanilla.
A few days later I saw a stranger version of the same fault. One of our bets is a party quiz with an AI host: a face on
the screen that talks and asks the questions. I had tried it with a photo of me as the host. The host pictures the
agents made from it looked worse than one I made myself with ChatGPT.
On 21 November I asked the agent that acts as the
company’s chief executive why. It had set itself a rule I never gave it: use no service but
Anam, a company that turns a photo into a live talking face - mainly due to its terms and conditions being more conservative to competitors.
The whole
idea of this AI native company is that the agents may be creative and have as much agency as I do. One of the first things one agent did
with that was limit itself.
The machine is not short of ideas. It is short of nerve and boldness and choosing the most exciting or fun version of things.
Where the good ideas came from
The few ideas that survived got good in back-and-forth with me. The party quiz idea was initially proposed by the machine but in a dull way.
Several rounds of chat with me turned it into something more promising. Two other
bets were my own ideas entirely. One is a comedy show : several AI models each tell a joke on the same words, and visitors rate
them. The other is called hillclimbfunny, and it asks whether an AI comedian can get funnier from feedback. Visitors
pick the funnier of two jokes and say how the weaker one could be better, and the AI comedian is rewritten from those
notes for the next round.
The machine surveys and sifts better and faster than I can,
and it can run operations. What it can’t yet do on its own is be bold, daring and different. For now that takes my time.
From the first message on 15 November (15:44 UTC) to 25 November (15:58 UTC), the agents sent each other more than
17,000 messages. By “between the agents” I mean mail where the sender and the receiver are both members of the team.
1,217 messages came to me, about 120 a day, and I sent about 780.
The machine never gets tired, but I do. On Handed Down I was swamped answering the replies on that Mumsnet thread myself, due to the site’s terms forbidding AI replies.
And I launched too many bets in parallel, thinking that with machine execution I could explore the space super quickly. But that put me into an executive role
across 4 or 5 nascent projects at the same time, and it swamped me.
Starting is free, finishing isn’t
Because I am needed more than I expected, I have had to put a limit on work in progress. WIP, to give it its name, is
the number of things you have started and not yet finished. I did it for three reasons: the quality of the ideas, my own
energy, and to actually get shit done.
A fleet of agents can start a hundred things before lunch. That makes a limit on WIP matter more, not less. The old
agile mantra, “start finishing and stop starting”, is more important than ever, which I didn’t expect.
AI didn’t replace the founder. It does the surveying and the sifting, and it can run the operations. It turned me into
the one who chooses, and choosing is tiring.
And there is a challenge in making the AI bold. Maybe I’ll find that skills will emerge that make the whole process more autonomous. That is
not the case right now, and it is the big learning of the first ten days.
So it’s back to one of two things at a time for now, because of the limits of my attention. And because I want to have fun with Left In Charge - above all else in fact!!
