AI credit default swaps have blown out. The credit behind the AI buildout is being repriced, and the value of every lease and guarantee is falling with it.
Groundbreaker Nov 20, 2026 720 146 Share In November 2008, John Paulson explained to Congress how his firm had made about $15 billion in 2007. He said the firm had become “very concerned about weak credit underwriting standards, excessive leverage among financial institutions, and a fundamental mispricing of credit risk.”
While the trade is usually remembered as a bet against the housing market, it was fundamentally a bet against the accuracy of the credit ratings. The rating agencies, and the banks, investors and insurers who relied on them, had deeply underestimated credit risk, and the leverage built on that mistake left the financial system fragile.
Moody’s and S&P rated riskier slices of subprime mortgage bonds as investment grade, relying on historical data from a period when home prices only rose and borrower defaults were uncorrelated. CDO managers pooled those slices and let diversification math turn most of them into triple-A. Bond insurers such as MBIA and Ambac guaranteed many of the securities, and any bond carrying their guarantee took on their triple-A rating.
Because the paper was rated investment grade, it could be financed as investment grade. Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers ran at more than thirty times leverage, funded overnight in the repo market, and every layer carried only the equity that a near-zero default rate required. The mispricing began with the ratings. Leverage turned it into a crisis.
That is why Paulson did not need every borrower to default. He needed only the market to price the chance of default correctly . Every balance sheet holding the bonds had been levered as if that chance were small, so when the market repriced the risk, it repriced the assets, and the thin equity beneath them went first. His first credit fund returned about 590% in 2007, well before most borrowers defaulted.
What led that repricing was the ABX index. Launched in January 2006, it gave subprime mortgage bonds - which almost never traded - a daily public price, with dealers quoting credit default swaps on every rated slice from AAA to BBB-.
The first crack in the index came on February 7, 2007, when banking giant HSBC announced severe losses from its U.S. subprime portfolio, causing the riskier tranches of the ABX index to plunge from about 97 at launch to 62. By the summer of 2007, the panic had spread to the index’s AAA-rated tranches - securities previously deemed as safe as government debt. The rating agencies began mass downgrades in July, and the stock market kept climbing until October.
That September, after the downgrades began, a Moody’s managing director said internally that the agency’s errors made it look “either incompetent at credit analysis, or like we sold our soul to the devil for revenue.”
Most of the market read the ABX as a gauge of sentiment - a sign that investors were nervous about subprime. But it was something much more important: the ABX was the first public measure of how far the ratings had understated the risk and therefore what the bonds, and the leverage built on them, were actually worth.
Gary Gorton, the Yale economist who studied the panic as it unfolded, explained why the index mattered: “For the first time information about subprime values and risks was aggregated and revealed.” The losses were already in the loans; the index made them visible. Once repo lenders could see the collateral deteriorating, they pulled back on funding, margin calls followed, and the liquidity crisis began.
The AI buildout already has an equivalent price signal. It shows what the ABX showed in 2007: a fundamental mispricing of credit risk.
Much of the AI build sits off-balance sheet, in leased data center campuses that rarely trade and have no public price. What does trade is the credit of the companies standing behind them.
OpenAI, Anthropic and the neoclouds could not finance campuses on their own credit, so the debt was made bankable by renting a stronger name: Oracle as lessee of record on the Stargate campuses, NVIDIA guaranteeing OpenAI’s lease in Ohio, Google backstopping Fluidstack’s leases for Anthropic, Broadcom standing behind Anthropic’s chip vehicles, and Meta writing residual value guarantees on its own off-balance-sheet campuses. Like the mortgage bond insurers, these companies rent their credit to obligations that could not earn it on their own.
Broadcom, NVIDIA and Google did it to sell more chips, Oracle to buy its way into the AI boom, and Meta to keep its own build off its balance sheet. This bit of financial engineering turned speculative frontier-lab demand into investment-grade debt.
Nearly every payer in the chain has a five-year credit default swap that trades daily. A credit default swap is insurance on a company’s debt: the buyer pays an annual premium, quoted in basis points (bp) of the amount insured, and the seller pays out if the company defaults. At Oracle’s 316bp, it costs about $316,000 a year to insure $10 million of Oracle debt for five years.
Since the end of June, the swaps have blown out. Broadcom’s swap is up about 155%, NVIDIA’s 130%, Oracle’s 80% and Meta’s 60%, and CoreWeave’s has roughly doubled. The CDX index of investment-grade credit, by contrast, has barely moved over the past year. This is a repricing of AI credit, and of little else.
A swap translates into two numbers that matter to investors:
The first is a default probability. The swap market assumes lenders recover about 40 cents on the dollar after a default, so the annual premium divided by 60% is roughly the market’s annual default rate. Oracle’s 76bp a year ago implied about a 6% chance of default over five years. Today, its 316bp implies about 23%, and CoreWeave’s 1,145bp about 61%.
The second is a market-implied credit rating . Moody’s has published market-implied ratings like this for two decades: find the median swap at each rating notch, and draw the boundaries between notches at the geometric mean of neighboring medians. Oracle’s 23% probability of default is that of a typical B+ company, and CoreWeave’s 61% puts it at CCC-. Moody’s says changes in these ratings usually lead changes in its own, and academic work agrees that swaps widen ahead of downgrades. On this grid, investment grade ends at about 135bp.
Run every lessee and guarantor in the build through the grid, and every rated payer with a traded swap sits four to seven notches below its agency rating, 5.3 on average .
Microsoft, rated AAA, trades as A+. Meta, rated AA-, as BBB-. NVIDIA, upgraded to AA by S&P in June, trades as BBB-, about 6bp from high yield. Broadcom, SpaceX and Oracle, all rated investment grade, trade as high yield. CoreWeave, rated B+, trades as CCC-.
The CDX’s 125 investment-grade credits, most of them triple-B, read as single-A on the same grid. The broad market trades at or above its agency ratings; the AI names alone trade far below theirs.
Some of the widening is hedging: banks with construction loan exposure to campuses buying protection to free capital, and bondholders hedging bonds they cannot sell without moving the price. That flow pushes the swap wider than a pure estimate of default would. It also makes the swap the price at which the risk clears: a hedger holds the risk and has decided to pay to be rid of it, and what it pays is what the risk costs. The same argument was made about the ABX. In March 2007, the New York Fed’s markets chief said its widening “was probably exaggerated.”
Most of the AI build’s swaps did not exist in any size until lenders to the build needed a hedge. CoreWeave’s began trading in late September 2025, Meta’s around its $30 billion bond sale at the end of October, and NVIDIA’s in November. The ABX first broke thirteen months after it launched. Thirteen months after these swaps began trading, every one of them is near its record.
Against all of this, the agencies have moved once: S&P cut Oracle from BBB to BBB- on July 9. Every other agency rating in the chain is where it was a year ago, or higher : S&P upgraded NVIDIA to AA in June. In fourteen months, Oracle’s swap-implied rating has fallen eight notches, from A to B+, while S&P has moved its rating down one.
Agency ratings lag by design: a downgrade can set off collateral calls and forced selling, so an agency that moves first risks causing what it describes. The result is ratings that are stable but slow. That stability is what can make ratings useful for deciding which index the bond sits in, how much capital an insurer must hold against it, or when a collateral clause springs. The swap carries none of those constraints, which makes it the most independent, real-time read on a company’s credit.
Henry Paulson, Treasury secretary through the crisis and no relation to John, told the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission in May 2010 that the agencies had been “critical to the functioning of the financial markets” and “failed us.” Reliance on their ratings, he said, was “just a crutch, and a dangerous crutch.”
III. A Double-B Credit, Financed as Single-A
Judging each name on its own understates the problem, because the obligations are concentrated in the names whose swaps have moved most. A rating for the buildout as a whole weights each name by what it has committed in debt, signed leases and guarantees, averages their five-year default probabilities, and converts the result back through the grid.
At the agency ratings, the roughly $2.2 trillion of rated obligations average out to a single-A credit, with about 5% of the weight in high yield and a weighted default probability near 5%. At the swaps, that probability rises to about 13%, which converts to BB+, with 30% of the weight below investment grade.
A standard structural model, which treats each lab’s latest valuation as its equity and the non-cancelable compute it has committed to buy as its debt, puts Anthropic at BB and OpenAI at BB-. Add those commitments and the total rises to about $3.5 trillion. The build’s rating falls to BB, with more than half of the weight in high yield.
On the market-implied ratings, the AI build is a double-B credit financed as if it were single-A.
The AI build carries the default risk of a high-yield credit, yet it was valued and levered as if its investment-grade agency ratings measured that risk correctly. This is Paulson’s fundamental mispricing of credit risk. It borrows at the agency rating while its risk trades at the swap, and that spread is the subsidy the build has run on.
IV. Where Wall Street Stood in 2007
Wall Street’s broker-dealers showed the same gap in 2007. At the end of February, Moody’s market-implied ratings put Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and Merrill Lynch five notches below their agency ratings, and Lehman Brothers and Bear Stearns four, an average of 4.6. By late July, Bear, Lehman, Goldman and Merrill all traded as high yield, six to seven notches below agency ratings that had not moved, for an average of 6.5. The AI build’s 5.3 sits between those two readings, roughly where Wall Street stood in the spring of 2007.
The companies behind the AI build are not funded overnight at thirty times leverage, as the broker-dealers were. But at 316bp, Oracle’s swap already trades wider than Bear Stearns’s did on January 1, 2008. Bear was rescued about eight months after its market-implied rating first fell to high yield. Merrill was sold and Lehman failed about fourteen months after they fell to high yield, each still rated investment grade. Oracle’s swap first crossed into high yield in early December 2025, eleven months ago.
